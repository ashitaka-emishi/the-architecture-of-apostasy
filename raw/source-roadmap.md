# Source Roadmap

Purpose: track sources and bibliography work needed to strengthen public essays without editing the essays prematurely.

## Historical Theology

### Constantine, The Edict Of Milan, And Legalization

Claims to support:

- Constantine and Licinius extended legal toleration and property restoration to Christians in 313.
- The 313 settlement legalized Christianity but did not make Nicene Christianity the official religion of the empire.
- The later move from toleration to imperial Christianity requires separate treatment and should not be collapsed into a single "Constantine made Christianity the state religion" claim.

Candidate primary and sourcebook citations:

- Lactantius, *De Mortibus Persecutorum*, chs. 34-35, 48. Fordham Internet History Sourcebooks, "Edicts of Toleration 311/313 AD": https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/edict-milan.asp
- Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book 10, ch. 5. CCEL/NPNF, "Copy of Imperial Decrees Translated from the Roman Tongue": https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf201.iii.xvi.v.html

Rival-reading notes:

- Use "so-called Edict of Milan" when precision matters, because the surviving text is commonly treated as an imperial letter preserved by Lactantius and Eusebius rather than a recovered edict text.
- Distinguish Constantine's patronage and favor toward Christianity from Theodosius I's later establishment of Nicene Christianity as imperial orthodoxy.

### Imperial Christianity And Church-State Entanglement

Claims to support:

- Imperial patronage altered the church's public status, resources, and relation to power.
- Church-state entanglement can be read as protection, providential opening, corruption risk, or a mixed historical development depending on tradition and source.

Candidate sources:

- Eusebius, *Life of Constantine*, for a pro-Constantinian Christian imperial reading.
- Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book 10, for imperial favor toward churches and clergy after toleration.
- Lactantius, *De Mortibus Persecutorum*, for the anti-persecution and toleration frame around the Constantinian settlement.

Rival-reading notes:

- Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox, Anabaptist, and secular accounts will not evaluate "imperial Christianity" identically.
- Avoid treating imperial patronage as either pure apostasy or simple providence without naming the claim type and source base.

### The Great Schism

Claims to support:

- The 1054 mutual excommunications are a conventional marker, not the whole cause of East-West division.
- The schism involved theological, jurisdictional, liturgical, linguistic, cultural, and political tensions over time.
- Catholic, Orthodox, and secular histories emphasize different causal centers.

Candidate Catholic-facing sources:

- EWTN, "The Great Schism That Divided East and West": https://www.ewtn.com/catholicism/library/great-schism-that-divided-east-and-west-10794

Candidate Orthodox-facing sources:

- Orthodox Church in America, "The Orthodox Faith: The Great Schism": https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/church-history/eleventh-century/the-great-schism

Candidate secular or synthetic sources:

- Christian History Institute, "1054 The East-West Schism": https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/magazine/article/east-west-schism
- Britannica or comparable reference work for a non-confessional chronology and terminology check.

Rival-reading notes:

- Catholic accounts tend to frame the schism through communion with Rome, papal authority, and long-term East-West estrangement.
- Orthodox accounts often foreground papal jurisdictional claims, the Filioque, and conciliar/pentarchic order.
- Secular accounts often de-emphasize a single 1054 rupture and stress long processes, language, empire, crusading, and later memory.

### The Reformation

Claims to support:

- Luther's 1517 theses belong first to an indulgence controversy and academic disputation context.
- Protestant, Catholic, Radical Reformation, and Anabaptist readings disagree on whether the Reformation was necessary reform, rupture, doctrinal recovery, institutional rebellion, or an incomplete reform.
- Radical Reformation and Anabaptist sources must not be reduced to Luther, Calvin, or Zwingli.

Candidate Protestant/Reformation primary sources:

- Martin Luther, "Disputation on the Power and Efficacy of Indulgences" / *Ninety-Five Theses* (1517). Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/274
- Luther text and Latin/English comparison resources, where needed, through university or sourcebook editions such as Oxford/Taylor Editions: https://editions.mml.ox.ac.uk/editions/luther-95-theses/luther-95-theses-eng.shtml

Candidate Catholic primary and official sources:

- Council of Trent, Session 25, "Decree Concerning Indulgences." Papal Encyclicals edition: https://www.papalencyclicals.net/councils/trent.htm
- EWTN, "Twenty-Fifth Session of the Council of Trent," for a Catholic-facing English presentation of the decrees: https://www.ewtn.com/catholicism/library/twentyfifth-session-of-the-council-of-trent-1492

Candidate Radical Reformation / Anabaptist sources:

- *The Schleitheim Confession of Faith* (1527), translated by J. C. Wenger. New Orleans Baptist Theological Seminary PDF: https://www.nobts.edu/baptist-center-theology/confessions/Schleitheim_Confession_of_Faith.pdf
- GAMEO, "Schleitheim Confession," for Anabaptist/Mennonite encyclopedia context: https://gameo.org/index.php?title=Schleitheim_Confession
- Christian History Institute, "Schleitheim Confession," for an accessible historical overview: https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/study/module/schleitheim

Rival-reading notes:

- When public essays mention indulgences, iconoclasm, sacramental conflict, or institutional corruption, keep Protestant polemic, Catholic defense/reform, and Radical/Anabaptist critique visibly distinct.
- Do not describe "the Reformation" as one unified Protestant movement. Track Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Radical, and Anabaptist trajectories separately when claims require precision.
- Treat the church-state question as a live point of disagreement: magisterial reformers, Catholic reformers, and Anabaptists often differ sharply on coercion, sacraments, discipline, and civil authority.

## Biblical And Interpretive Sources

### Seven Churches In Revelation

Claims to support:

- Revelation 2-3 addresses seven historical assemblies in Asia Minor and also functions canonically as warning, exhortation, and promise for the wider church.
- Interpretive claims about the seven churches must distinguish direct textual observation, historical reconstruction, theological symbolism, and typological application.
- Strong claims that the seven churches map onto successive church ages require explicit argument and should not be treated as the default reading.

Biblical anchors:

- Revelation 1:9-20 for John's setting, the lampstands/stars symbolism, and the command to write.
- Revelation 2:1-3:22 for the seven messages.
- Revelation 1:11 for the named sequence: Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia, and Laodicea.

Candidate commentary and scholarly sources:

- G. K. Beale, *The Book of Revelation: A Commentary on the Greek Text* (NIGTC), for symbolic, intertextual, and canonical interpretation.
- Craig R. Koester, *Revelation and the End of All Things*, for accessible literary/theological interpretation.
- Craig R. Koester, *Revelation: A New Translation with Introduction and Commentary* (Anchor Yale Bible), for historical setting and detailed exegesis.
- David E. Aune, *Revelation 1-5* (Word Biblical Commentary), for historical-critical and textual detail on the seven messages.
- Colin J. Hemer, *The Letters to the Seven Churches of Asia in Their Local Setting*, for local historical and archaeological background.

Rival-reading notes:

- Historical-local reading: the letters address concrete first-century churches in Asia Minor.
- Canonical-symbolic reading: the seven churches represent recurring conditions, temptations, and judgments in the church.
- Church-age reading: the seven churches are read as a prophetic sequence of church history; this requires separate argument and should be marked as a contested interpretive model.
- Pastoral/exhortative reading: each message summons repentance, endurance, discernment, and hearing by the Spirit, without requiring a rigid historical timetable.

### Philadelphia And Laodicea

Claims to support:

- Philadelphia and Laodicea were historical churches addressed in Revelation 3.
- Public essays may use Philadelphia and Laodicea as theological symbols or typological warnings only when that move is marked as interpretation, not as a settled prophetic map.
- Laodicea's wealth, earthquake recovery, textile trade, and medical/eye-salve associations are common historical-background claims that need source support before being used strongly.

Biblical anchors:

- Revelation 3:7-13 for Philadelphia's "little strength," kept word, open door, endurance, and promise.
- Revelation 3:14-22 for Laodicea's wealth claim, lukewarmness, blindness/nakedness imagery, call to buy gold, white garments, and eye salve, and Christ at the door.

Candidate historical-background sources:

- Tacitus, *Annals* 14.27, for the claim that Laodicea recovered from earthquake damage by its own resources. Perseus/Tufts text: https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Tac.+Ann.+14.27
- Biblical Archaeology Society, "The Church of Laodicea in the Bible and Archaeology," for an accessible archaeology/background entry: https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-sites-places/biblical-archaeology-sites/church-of-laodicea-in-the-bible-and-archaeology/
- Hemer, *The Letters to the Seven Churches of Asia in Their Local Setting*, for local-setting treatment of Philadelphia and Laodicea.
- Koester and Aune commentaries on Revelation 3 for textual and historical caution around local allusions.

Rival-reading notes:

- Treat Philadelphia/Laodicea application to modern church history as typological warning unless a full historical case is added.
- Avoid deriving too much from name etymologies alone; use textual context and historical sources.
- Distinguish Laodicea's civic wealth and self-sufficiency from the spiritual diagnosis in Revelation 3. The analogy is suggestive but must not become automatic proof.

### Nehushtan, Typology, And Sacred-Symbol Corruption

Claims to support:

- The bronze serpent begins as a divinely commanded sign of healing in Numbers 21.
- 2 Kings 18:4 records Hezekiah destroying the bronze serpent because Israel had begun burning incense to it.
- John 3:14-15 rereads the lifted serpent typologically in relation to the lifting up of the Son of Man.
- The project's "Nehushtan" use is an interpretive pattern: holy gifts or signs can become idols when detached from God and trusted as controllable power.

Biblical anchors:

- Numbers 21:4-9 for the bronze serpent as a commanded sign in the wilderness.
- 2 Kings 18:1-8, especially 18:4, for Hezekiah's reform and the destruction of the bronze serpent called Nehushtan.
- John 3:14-15 for Christological typology of the lifted serpent.
- Isaiah 1:10-17, Amos 5:21-24, and Matthew 23 as broader warning texts about outward religious form detached from justice, repentance, and truth.

Candidate sources:

- Hebrew lexicon entries for `nechosheth` / bronze and `nachash` / serpent when explaining the wordplay behind "Nehushtan."
- Study Bible or commentary notes on Numbers 21, 2 Kings 18, and John 3 from at least two traditions before public essay use.
- Intertextual Bible, "Numbers 21:9 / 2 Kings 18:4," for a quick intertextual index: https://intertextual.bible/text/numbers-21.9/2-kings-18.4
- Major biblical commentaries on Numbers, Kings, and John for fuller support before essay-level footnotes.

Rival-reading notes:

- Some readers will treat the bronze serpent primarily as a historical cult object later abused; others will emphasize canonical typology fulfilled in Christ.
- The project should not imply that every symbol, sacrament, institution, or artifact is inherently idolatrous. The Nehushtan pattern concerns detachment from living trust in God.
- Christological use should move through John 3 carefully: the lifted serpent is fulfilled in the Son of Man being lifted up, not in a generic theory of sacred objects.
