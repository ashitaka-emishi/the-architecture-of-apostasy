# Source Roadmap

Purpose: track sources and bibliography work needed to strengthen public essays without editing the essays prematurely.

## Historical Theology

### Constantine, The Edict Of Milan, And Legalization

Claims to support:

- Constantine and Licinius extended legal toleration and property restoration to Christians in 313.
- The 313 settlement legalized Christianity but did not make Nicene Christianity the official religion of the empire.
- The later move from toleration to imperial Christianity requires separate treatment and should not be collapsed into a single "Constantine made Christianity the state religion" claim.

Candidate primary and sourcebook citations:

- Lactantius, *De Mortibus Persecutorum*, chs. 34-35, 48. Fordham Internet History Sourcebooks, "Edicts of Toleration 311/313 AD": https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/edict-milan.asp
- Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book 10, ch. 5. CCEL/NPNF, "Copy of Imperial Decrees Translated from the Roman Tongue": https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf201.iii.xvi.v.html

Rival-reading notes:

- Use "so-called Edict of Milan" when precision matters, because the surviving text is commonly treated as an imperial letter preserved by Lactantius and Eusebius rather than a recovered edict text.
- Distinguish Constantine's patronage and favor toward Christianity from Theodosius I's later establishment of Nicene Christianity as imperial orthodoxy.

### Imperial Christianity And Church-State Entanglement

Claims to support:

- Imperial patronage altered the church's public status, resources, and relation to power.
- Church-state entanglement can be read as protection, providential opening, corruption risk, or a mixed historical development depending on tradition and source.

Candidate sources:

- Eusebius, *Life of Constantine*, for a pro-Constantinian Christian imperial reading.
- Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book 10, for imperial favor toward churches and clergy after toleration.
- Lactantius, *De Mortibus Persecutorum*, for the anti-persecution and toleration frame around the Constantinian settlement.

Rival-reading notes:

- Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox, Anabaptist, and secular accounts will not evaluate "imperial Christianity" identically.
- Avoid treating imperial patronage as either pure apostasy or simple providence without naming the claim type and source base.

### The Great Schism

Claims to support:

- The 1054 mutual excommunications are a conventional marker, not the whole cause of East-West division.
- The schism involved theological, jurisdictional, liturgical, linguistic, cultural, and political tensions over time.
- Catholic, Orthodox, and secular histories emphasize different causal centers.

Candidate Catholic-facing sources:

- EWTN, "The Great Schism That Divided East and West": https://www.ewtn.com/catholicism/library/great-schism-that-divided-east-and-west-10794

Candidate Orthodox-facing sources:

- Orthodox Church in America, "The Orthodox Faith: The Great Schism": https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/church-history/eleventh-century/the-great-schism

Candidate secular or synthetic sources:

- Christian History Institute, "1054 The East-West Schism": https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/magazine/article/east-west-schism
- Britannica or comparable reference work for a non-confessional chronology and terminology check.

Rival-reading notes:

- Catholic accounts tend to frame the schism through communion with Rome, papal authority, and long-term East-West estrangement.
- Orthodox accounts often foreground papal jurisdictional claims, the Filioque, and conciliar/pentarchic order.
- Secular accounts often de-emphasize a single 1054 rupture and stress long processes, language, empire, crusading, and later memory.

### The Reformation

Claims to support:

- Luther's 1517 theses belong first to an indulgence controversy and academic disputation context.
- Protestant, Catholic, Radical Reformation, and Anabaptist readings disagree on whether the Reformation was necessary reform, rupture, doctrinal recovery, institutional rebellion, or an incomplete reform.
- Radical Reformation and Anabaptist sources must not be reduced to Luther, Calvin, or Zwingli.

Candidate Protestant/Reformation primary sources:

- Martin Luther, "Disputation on the Power and Efficacy of Indulgences" / *Ninety-Five Theses* (1517). Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/274
- Luther text and Latin/English comparison resources, where needed, through university or sourcebook editions such as Oxford/Taylor Editions: https://editions.mml.ox.ac.uk/editions/luther-95-theses/luther-95-theses-eng.shtml

Candidate Catholic primary and official sources:

- Council of Trent, Session 25, "Decree Concerning Indulgences." Papal Encyclicals edition: https://www.papalencyclicals.net/councils/trent.htm
- EWTN, "Twenty-Fifth Session of the Council of Trent," for a Catholic-facing English presentation of the decrees: https://www.ewtn.com/catholicism/library/twentyfifth-session-of-the-council-of-trent-1492

Candidate Radical Reformation / Anabaptist sources:

- *The Schleitheim Confession of Faith* (1527), translated by J. C. Wenger. New Orleans Baptist Theological Seminary PDF: https://www.nobts.edu/baptist-center-theology/confessions/Schleitheim_Confession_of_Faith.pdf
- GAMEO, "Schleitheim Confession," for Anabaptist/Mennonite encyclopedia context: https://gameo.org/index.php?title=Schleitheim_Confession
- Christian History Institute, "Schleitheim Confession," for an accessible historical overview: https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/study/module/schleitheim

Rival-reading notes:

- When public essays mention indulgences, iconoclasm, sacramental conflict, or institutional corruption, keep Protestant polemic, Catholic defense/reform, and Radical/Anabaptist critique visibly distinct.
- Do not describe "the Reformation" as one unified Protestant movement. Track Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Radical, and Anabaptist trajectories separately when claims require precision.
- Treat the church-state question as a live point of disagreement: magisterial reformers, Catholic reformers, and Anabaptists often differ sharply on coercion, sacraments, discipline, and civil authority.

## Biblical And Interpretive Sources

- Interpretive approaches to the seven churches in Revelation.
- Philadelphia and Laodicea as historical churches, theological symbols, and typological warnings.
- Nehushtan, typology, idolatry, and sacred-symbol corruption.

## Modern Institutional Sources

- Church-growth models and megachurch metrics.
- Consumer religion and market-shaped ecclesiology.
- 501(c)(3), nonprofit law, donor systems, church incorporation, and religious tax exemption.

## Constructive Examples

- Faithful institutional forms across Christian traditions.
- Pastoral authority with accountability.
- Sacramental, liturgical, congregational, and missional forms that preserve gifts without idolizing them.
