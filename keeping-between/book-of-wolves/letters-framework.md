# Framework for the Book of Wolves Letters

## Current controlling design

The active proposed literary design is now **The Book of Wolves: Letters Concerning the Keeping of the Troth**, recorded in `book-of-wolves/BOOK_DESIGN.md` and mapped in `book-of-wolves/recipient-register.json`.

This framework remains useful for epistolary method and doctrinal pressure, but its older loose sequence is superseded by the twelve-letter Book of Wolves form: eleven letters from Andren and a final corrective letter from Little Vey to Andren. The Book-of-Wolves naming of **Andren** applies to this correspondence workstream and does not revise the accepted *Aru Va'en* narration, where the narrator's personal name remains unstated.

## Governing design rule

The letters must remain **epistolary situations rather than miniature doctrinal essays**.

Their theology should emerge through grief, institutional conflict, failed guardianship, resentment, fear, disputed authority, estrangement, healing, belonging, endurance, reconciliation, and return.

Vaerun should not begin as an infallible doctrinal voice. He should remain correctable, capable of confession, and increasingly aware that his own authority can become institutionalized.

The collection should enact its theology.

## Established continuity: Little Vey

Little Vey survives the Keeping as a living, embodied companion and later writes to Vaerun. His letter is therefore part of the post-Keeping correspondence, not a memorial reconstruction or a spectral voice.

The frozen source letter `source/book-of-the-troth/The Letter of Little Vey to the Vaerun.md` warns Vaerun about stature, fame, indispensability, and the throne others may build beneath a keeper. It argues that smallness can disclose cries, wounds, and places hidden from strength. The active revision at `book-of-wolves/letters/LITTLE_VEY_TO_VAERUN.md` centers the transition from **a Vaerun**, one bearer of the crossing vocation, to **the Vaerun**, the singular public stature made by the many after the hill. Vey summons him to remember where the companions stood, when only they knew his name, where the Shepherd stood, and the Troth. Its presence supplies a living corrective voice; Vaerun's response and the letter's final placement remain open.

The source statement that Vaerun did not then call himself `Vaerun` does not control the active edition because the definitive myth already makes him **a Vaerun** before the hill. The active letter remembers that only his companions knew his name then, before the many made him **the Vaerun** after the hill. The definite title records accrued stature, not a new calling.

## Doctrinal axes available to the letters

| Question | Framework |
|---|---|
| What am I responsible for keeping? | Vaelun / Vaerun / Hrovan |
| What happens when role becomes identity? | Aruvan |
| Does suffering or vulnerability make me righteous? | Living Lamb |
| How do we judge claims to faithfulness? | Troth |
| Who finally holds keeper and kept? | Shepherd |
| How can I act without claiming righteousness? | Unclaimed Virtue |

Each letter should place at least two of these into tension rather than simply explaining one.

## I. Letters of Keeping

### Letter to a Vaelun

Situation: a guardian has defended a boundary successfully for so long that every stranger is now interpreted as danger.

Core tension:

> **A gate that can no longer open has ceased to be a gate.**

The letter must not abolish boundaries. Some crossings should be refused. The problem is discernment without sacralized exclusion.

### Letter to a Hrovan

Situation: something is wrong within the hall, but telling the truth may weaken the community.

Core question:

> Does fidelity require preserving the hall, or telling the truth that may wound it?

This makes “Before throne and pasture” concrete.

### Letter to a Vaerun

Situation: a boundary-crosser has come to treat crossing itself as proof of courage and fidelity.

Core question:

> When does crossing cease to serve the Troth and become another form of self-justification?

No guardian office is morally self-validating.

## II. Letters of Fang and Fleece

These should include paired or neighboring letters.

### Letter of the Fang

Situation: a guardian harmed someone while sincerely trying to protect the community.

Defense:

> *I did what had to be done.*

Theological pressure: responsibility and sacrifice do not establish virtue.

### Letter of the Fleece

Situation: one genuinely wounded by that guardian begins to treat the wound as final moral authority.

Temptation:

> *Because I was wronged, I alone may determine what justice requires.*

Theology:

> **The fang is not condemned because it is fang.  
> The fleece is not justified because it is fleece.**

Both stand before the Shepherd, who judges every claim made in the name of the Troth.

## III. Letters Concerning the Long Wolf

Move from individual pathology to institutional memory.

Situation: an ancient guardian order maintains questionable practices by invoking those who suffered and died keeping the old covenant.

Its argument:

> *If what we keep no longer matters, what did our fathers suffer for?*

The Aruvan appears as accumulated keeping: memory, sacrifice, oath, and responsibility taking institutional body.

Vaerun should learn during this correspondence rather than simply possess the answer from the beginning.

A later correction may include:

> *I told you to bear what I should have helped you relinquish.*

This allows the Doctrine of Unclaimed Virtue to apply to Vaerun himself.

The saying may emerge here:

> **Even the keeper must be kept.  
> Nothing faithful keeps alone.**

## IV. The Letter Concerning the Troth

Only after the reader encounters concrete conflicts should the collection receive a major circular letter.

Situation: communities have begun using “The Troth remains” in contradictory ways.

Possible distortions:

- “Our traditions must never change.”
- “Nothing binds us because the Troth is beyond institutions.”
- “Reconciliation means justice should be set aside.”
- “Remembering injury means reconciliation is betrayal.”

The major clarification:

> **The Troth remaining does not mean everything else must remain.**

Possible formulation:

> **Because the Troth remains, the throne may fall.  
> Because the Troth remains, the pasture may be left.  
> Because the Troth remains, the fang may be laid down.  
> Because the Troth remains, the fleece need not remain wounded forever.**

## V. Letters of the Shepherd

The Shepherd should become fully explicit relatively late.

If introduced too early as the answer to everything, the correspondence risks becoming devotional instruction rather than tested theology.

Situation: an exhausted guardian cannot stop because others depend upon him.

Vaerun must not say “be stronger.”

He identifies the hidden assumption:

> *You have mistaken exhaustion for fidelity because you fear that whatever leaves your hands leaves all keeping.*

The deeper discovery:

> **The Shepherd was already there.**

Relinquishment becomes a theological act.

## VI. Letters of Return

These letters should concern actual betrayal, harm, and failed offices rather than abstract sin.

Possible situations:

- a failed Hrovan;
- a guardian who used violence wrongly;
- a Living Lamb whose grievance hardened into vengeance;
- one who abandoned a boundary;
- someone Vaerun himself once condemned.

Central question:

> **What does return actually require?**

The answer must avoid:

- pretending nothing happened;
- automatic restoration to former office;
- compulsory forgiveness;
- permanent exile.

The fallen guardian may return without immediately becoming guardian again.

The wounded may forgive without surrendering discernment.

The community may welcome without forgetting.

The Troth remains, but the restored relationship may have a different form.

## VII. Vaerun's Last Letter

Late in the collection, Vaerun confronts the institutionalization of his own authority.

People quote his letters.

His judgments become precedents.

They begin asking:

> *What would Vaerun say?*

instead of:

> *What does the Troth require?*

Vaerun recognizes the Aruvan problem forming around himself.

His final act is relinquishment.

Working formulation:

> *If you keep my words because they are mine, you have misunderstood them. Test what I have said. Correct what I have failed to see. Keep nothing merely because Vaerun kept it.*

The book thereby enacts Unclaimed Virtue rather than merely teaching it.

## VIII. Vaerun Does Not Get the Final Word

After the last letter should come replies, remembered sayings, communal testimony, or multiple voices.

Little Vey's active letter to Vaerun supplies at least one living companion voice within the collection, whether it appears here or earlier. The collection should preserve its correction rather than paraphrase it into a teaching attributed back to Vaerun.

Someone may correct Vaerun.

Someone may remember him differently.

Two companions may disagree about what he meant.

The collection need not resolve every disagreement.

Its final confession need not be:

> *Vaerun was right.*

It can simply be:

> **The Troth remains.**

## Overall progression

**Guardianship**  
→ What have I been entrusted to keep?

**Fang and Fleece**  
→ Does my position confer righteousness?

**Aruvan and Living Lamb**
→ What happens when generations of keeping and being kept become paired identities?

**Troth**  
→ What remains when roles, institutions, and identities fail?

**Shepherd**  
→ Who keeps when created keepers cannot?

**Return**  
→ Can the fallen re-enter relation without denying judgment?

**Vaerun's relinquishment**  
→ Can even the teacher surrender the authority created by his own teaching?

The entire collection should be structured so that even its author eventually stands beneath the confession:

**Before throne and pasture.  
Before fang and fleece.  
The Troth remains.**
