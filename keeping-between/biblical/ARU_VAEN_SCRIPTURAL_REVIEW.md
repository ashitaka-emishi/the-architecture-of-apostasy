# Aru Va'en Scriptural Review

## Purpose

This dossier tested and constrained the revision of *Aru Va'en* before its acceptance under D-090 and remains the controlling research record for later interpretation. It is not a list of verses that resemble the myth, and it does not treat Troth vocabulary as biblical vocabulary. Its work is to distinguish what Scripture says from what the project constructs and to keep every inferential step visible.

The user remains the final theological and literary authority. This dossier may recommend, constrain, or reject a proposed reading, but it cannot promote a proposal to `user_established` or accept the final myth on the user's behalf.

The following user-established decisions control this review:

- Christ is the measure; the Troth must not become a second Logos, deity, or autonomous moral principle.
- The frozen PDF is definitive over the recovered V3 draft.
- *Aru Va'en* remains the central symbolic narrative, with the Shepherd at its Christological center.
- The myth should itself perform a keeper's vocation while remaining kept and judged by the Shepherd.
- The myth must retain its old, oral, liturgical register.

## Source conflicts this review must resolve or constrain

1. **Christ or Troth as measure:** current decisions and `Troth_Christology.md` place the Troth beneath Christ, while parts of `the-shepherd-who-keeps.md` say that the Troth judges the Shepherd or supplies his authority. The review must correct the latter without collapsing divine faithfulness into arbitrary command.
2. **Primordial language:** the PDF begins with the Troth and an ache, but closes with the Shepherd before the Troth, the cry before the vow, and love before grief. The review must distinguish narrative sequence from metaphysical priority.
3. **Shepherd, individual lamb, and Living Lamb:** Christian Scripture identifies Christ through both Shepherd and Lamb images. The hill-lamb must remain creaturely, and the Living Lamb as the kept-and-gathered face of the communion also seen in Aruvan must not become a second Christ, a rival saving body, or a sanctification of passive suffering.
4. **Judgment and return:** the revision must open return to fallen persons without turning mercy into denial, restoration to office, or release from consequences.
5. **Resistance and force:** biblical non-retaliation, enemy-love, protection, public authority, restraint, and judgment must not be compressed into a slogan that either sanctifies violence or requires passivity before harm. D-030 fixes a constructive least-harm choreography without presenting it as the uncontested lexical meaning of a biblical passage.
6. **Morning and wounds:** the PDF's restored morning risks erasing death and injury. The revision needs resurrection-shaped hope without claiming that the myth depicts literal resurrection or making morning a cheap reset.
7. **Staff imported from V3:** V3 adds a Shepherd's staff that is absent from the definitive PDF. D-026 rejects the import; active keeping must be embodied without a fantasy-wizard or armed-guardian prop.
8. **Aruvan spelling:** earlier active development material treated `Aruvann` as a separate commentary spelling. D-027 identifies it as a misspelling of source `Aruvan`; no second being, office, or category may be inferred from the extra letter.
9. **A Vaerun or the Vaerun:** the definitive myth gives the narrator the crossing office before the hill, while the recovered Little Vey letter sometimes speaks as though he did not yet call himself Vaerun. D-029 controls the active continuity: he was a Vaerun before the hill and became known as the Vaerun afterward, when public memory made his shared vocation into singular stature.

## Claim classes

Every substantive entry uses the project's existing classes:

- **B1 - Textual / lexical:** the passage's wording and literary function.
- **B2 - Historical-critical:** responsible claims about historical, social, political, or religious context.
- **B3 - Canonical-theological:** a larger Christian canonical pattern that exceeds the immediate passage.
- **B4 - Constructive theological:** a Troth synthesis built from, but not identical to, B1-B3.
- **B5 - Mythopoetic:** a proposed symbolic or narrative use in *Aru Va'en*.
- **B6 - Liturgical / pastoral:** a proposed communal confession, practice, or pastoral implication.

## Evidence-entry format

Each major passage or passage cluster must record:

1. **Reference and translation:** identify the biblical text and distinguish quotation from paraphrase.
2. **B1 textual claim:** state what the passage addresses in its literary context.
3. **B2 context:** include only historically supportable claims.
4. **Rival readings:** preserve material translation, historical, and theological alternatives.
5. **B3 canonical synthesis:** state the wider Christian reading and acknowledge where it exceeds B1.
6. **B4 constructive inference:** show the reasoning that connects the passage to a Troth claim.
7. **B5 mythic constraint or permission:** state what the myth must, may, or must not do.
8. **B6 liturgical or pastoral use:** include only where warranted.
9. **Misuse test:** identify readings that could enable domination, passivity, self-certification, cheap mercy, or erased harm.
10. **Confidence and unresolved tension:** do not disguise open questions as settled exegesis.
11. **Sources:** give exact bibliographic data and page or stable section information for scholarship.

## I. Christological spine

### Governing research conclusion

The Shepherd should be a recognizable mythopoetic presentation of Jesus Christ, not an ideal guardian who merely happens to resemble him. Recognition should arise from the myth's images and actions rather than an opening label. **Vey's ending names his priority rather than unmasking him.** The canonical convergence is stronger than a generic pastoral metaphor:

- John 1 and Colossians 1 place the incarnate Son on the divine side of creation and identify him as the personal disclosure of God.
- Ezekiel 34 joins divine shepherding, judgment of failed rulers, judgment within the flock, and hope for one Davidic shepherd.
- John 9-10 presents Jesus as the gate and Good Shepherd who knows, calls, leads, gathers, lays down his life, and takes it up again.
- Philippians 2, Mark 10, and John 13 identify Christ-shaped lordship with self-giving service rather than status exploitation or domination.
- Revelation 5 and 7 unite Lamb, throne, victory, worship, and shepherding; the slain Lamb stands and acts.
- John 20 and 1 Corinthians 15 hold transformed life together with embodied continuity, while Revelation 21 places final renewal in God's action.

**B3 canonical synthesis:** The Shepherd is not one keeper within a system governed by the Troth. He is the incarnate, crucified, risen, and reigning Christ under whose personal lordship every account of fidelity, keeping, judgment, mercy, and return must stand.

**B4 constructive consequence:** The project may say that the Shepherd keeps the keepers and reveals the form of faithful keeping. It must not say that an independent Troth authorizes, judges, or morally precedes Christ.

**B5 mythic consequence:** The Shepherd must act as the story's center. Wounded presence is essential, but silence and endurance alone are insufficient. He must search, know, call, make room, gather, judge, protect, receive, and lead toward life without becoming a warrior-guardian or an abstract voice of approval. These Christological actions should be legible from his first appearance without explanatory exposition or a final-act unmasking.

### A. The Word before every created principle

#### Primary texts

- John 1:1-18
- Colossians 1:12-20
- Hebrews 1:1-4
- John 14:6-11

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

John's prologue places the Logos "in the beginning," relates the Logos both to God and as God, assigns creation through him, and then identifies the Logos with the one who became flesh and makes the Father known. The prologue's literary movement is not from an abstract standard to its best human example; it is from the divine Word through whom creation exists to the Word's embodied presence in Jesus.

The language of "grace and truth" in John 1:14 may echo the covenantal qualities of Exodus 34:6. That resonance permits a connection between Christ and divine fidelity, but it does not establish a lexical equivalence between `Troth`, Greek `logos`, Greek `aletheia`, Hebrew `emet`, or any single biblical term. The [NABRE notes on John 1](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/john/1) identify creation, Wisdom, incarnation, tabernacle, glory, and covenant fidelity as overlapping backgrounds.

Colossians 1:15-20 presents Christ as image of the invisible God, before all things, the one through and for whom all things were created, the one in whom they hold together, firstborn from the dead, and agent of reconciliation through the cross. The passage's poetic or hymnic character strengthens rather than weakens its relevance to an old liturgical myth: it places a concentrated Christological confession inside communal discourse. The [NABRE notes on Colossians 1](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/colossians/1) describe a creation movement and a redemption movement joined in Christ.

John 14:6 does not present truth as an impersonal reality standing above Jesus. Within Johannine discourse, Jesus identifies himself with way, truth, and life, and the surrounding exchange relates knowing Jesus to knowing the Father. The [NABRE note on John 14:6](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/john/14) describes truth here as the Father's reality manifested in Jesus' person and works.

#### B2 - Historical and literary questions

- John 1:1-18 may incorporate or adapt hymnic material. Its relation to Genesis, Jewish Wisdom traditions, and Hellenistic `logos` discourse is debated.
- Colossians authorship is disputed, as are the prehistory, stanza structure, and possible redaction of 1:15-20.
- "Firstborn of all creation" can be misunderstood as making Christ the first creature; within the immediate passage, however, the following clauses assign all created orders through and for him and place him before all things.
- Whether Colossians 1:20's reconciliation of "all things" implies universal salvation, cosmic restoration, pacification of powers, or another form of cosmic ordering is disputed. It cannot be made to settle the myth's return mechanism by itself.

#### Rival readings that must remain visible

1. **Wisdom/Logos backgrounds:** interpreters differ over the relative weight of Jewish Wisdom, divine-word, and Hellenistic philosophical backgrounds in John 1. The project needs only the high-confidence literary claim that the evangelist identifies the preexistent creative Logos with the incarnate Jesus.
2. **Colossians and the powers:** some read reconciliation as positive restored relation; others include defeat or pacification of hostile powers. Janusz Krecidlo argues for creation completed through reconciliation rather than mere pacification, while the passage's universal language remains debated.
3. **Systematic reach:** Nicene claims are canonically and doctrinally relevant to the project, but the dossier must not pretend that later technical formulations are the lexical content of any one verse.

#### B3 - Canonical synthesis

John 1, John 14, Colossians 1, and Hebrews 1 together support a Christian reading in which God's creative, revelatory, sustaining, and reconciling work is inseparable from the Son. No created or abstract fidelity principle can stand alongside him as a second source or measure.

#### B4 - Constructive inference

The Troth may name fidelity to the Good only if "the Good" is not left more ultimate or more determinate than Christ. A stronger layered formulation is:

> **The Troth names the fidelity of God disclosed and enacted in Jesus Christ, and the creaturely calling to participate in that fidelity by the Spirit.**

This is a user-accepted constructive formulation under D-073, not a biblical definition of the word *troth*. It preserves objectivity because fidelity is grounded in God's character; it preserves Christological priority because Christ is not measured by an independent ideal; and it preserves creaturely responsibility because participation can be faithful or false.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- "Before the Troth ... there was the Shepherd" should remain.
- "Before" should name personal and theological priority, not a temporal interval in which Christ existed before God became faithful.
- The opening ache may be creaturely apprehension of broken relation or the cry that precedes a creature's vow. It must not be a pre-divine substance, force, or moral law.
- The myth may show the Troth being recognized, remembered, confessed, betrayed, or kept. It must not personify the Troth as a speaker or agent independent of the Shepherd.
- Existing doctrinal sentences saying that the Troth judges the Shepherd or supplies his authority require revision. Christ's character and work judge every human use of Troth language.

#### Misuse test

This synthesis fails if it:

- turns Christ into an illustration of a prior moral system;
- turns "Christ is the measure" into permission for a leader to identify personal commands with Christ;
- treats the myth's Shepherd as exhaustive of Christ rather than a bounded mythic witness;
- claims that *Troth* is the true lexical meaning of `logos`, `aletheia`, `emet`, `pistis`, or another biblical word.

### B. The divine and Davidic Shepherd

#### Primary texts

- Ezekiel 34
- John 9:35-10:30
- Psalm 23
- Hebrews 13:20-21
- 1 Peter 5:1-4

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

Ezekiel 34 first indicts Israel's shepherds for feeding themselves, failing to strengthen the weak, heal the sick, bind the injured, seek the lost, and gather the scattered. God then declares that God will personally search for and shepherd the flock. The chapter also judges between members of the flock: strong sheep can foul pasture and drive weaker sheep away. Finally, it promises one Davidic shepherd. The chapter therefore refuses both ruler innocence and flock innocence.

John 10 follows the healing and expulsion of the man born blind in John 9. The discourse contrasts voices and forms of leadership while presenting Jesus as both gate and shepherd. He calls sheep by name, leads them out, gives abundant life, knows and is known, gathers other sheep, lays down his life, and takes it up again. The discourse presents a relation of recognition and divine mission, not a generic theory of benevolent ownership. See the [NRSVUE text of John 10:1-18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+10%3A1-18&version=NRSVUE) and [NABRE notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/john/10).

Christopher W. Skinner argues that a shepherd dying for the sheep exceeds ordinary pastoral realism and is the discourse's singular Christological element. This cautions the project against treating the Shepherd's self-giving as merely what every sufficiently brave guardian ought to do.

#### B2 - Historical and literary questions

- The exact structure and beginning of John 10, its relation to John 9, and the development of its imagery remain disputed.
- Ezekiel 34's shepherds represent leaders, with royal and broader governing connotations. The chapter addresses an exilic crisis and failed leadership before it becomes Christian Christological material.
- The identity of John 10's "other sheep" is debated: Gentiles, dispersed children of God, groups divided within Johannine Christianity, or a deliberately widening horizon.
- Andreas J. Kostenberger traces the convergence of divine shepherding, Davidic expectation, and gathering traditions in John 10. His historical-Jesus and community conclusions are not the only scholarly account, but the Ezekiel-David-John intertext is well grounded.

#### Rival and corrective readings

1. John 10 can be read primarily as commentary on John 9's conflict, as a messianic fulfillment of Ezekiel, as community boundary discourse, or through some combination.
2. The polemic against authorities in John must not become anti-Jewish narration. Adele Reinhartz documents how John's language has contributed to Christian anti-Judaism. The myth must oppose false shepherding without assigning corruption to an ethnicity, people, or religious ancestry.
3. "One flock" cannot authorize coercive uniformity. The shepherd gathers through voice and recognition, not by turning the fold into an absolute institution.

#### B3 - Canonical synthesis

The canonical Shepherd joins divine initiative and messianic embodiment: God seeks, heals, gathers, judges, and provides life; Jesus takes up this work through personal knowledge, self-giving death, resurrection agency, and gathering beyond the existing fold.

#### B4 - Constructive inference

The Shepherd's sovereignty is categorically different from a guardian's entrusted office, but not because action has been replaced by passive suffering. His keeping is active, personal, discerning, self-giving, life-producing, and free from the need to consume the flock or preserve an institution.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- The Shepherd must visibly do more than stand and absorb wounds.
- Fitting actions include searching, naming, binding, healing, making room, opening a way, gathering, distinguishing, receiving, and leading.
- During the assault, the Shepherd progressively heals the creaturely lamb within the circle through recognizable bodily care: supporting breath, cleaning wounds, binding them with his torn robe, steadying the lamb with scarred hands, and continuing to call and calm it. The efficacy quietly exceeds ordinary first aid as bleeding stops, breathing steadies, flesh begins to close, and strength returns, but there is no spectacle or instant restoration. As it heals, the lamb begins to remember what it is: while still wounded in his hands, its ear and then its head turn toward his voice. Breath, posture, strength, and chosen companionship grow from this attention so bodily restoration manifests renewed creaturely relation and proper ordering beneath the Shepherd. His prior care sustains response rather than rewarding it, and incomplete cure does not imply sin, failed faith, lesser worth, or disordered relation. The wounds neither transfer to the Shepherd nor purchase healing. Receiving their reality deepens his grief, and that grief strengthens the circle as room and steadiness rather than healing mechanism or combat power.
- The Shepherd sorts within bodies rather than between persons. He separates without severing and reorders each member caught in an imposed curse-mark within the whole body beneath himself: fang away from throat and toward protection; eye opened to depth and truth; paw restored from false cleanliness to creaturely texture, weight, and responsibility; mouth loosened so cry and truthful answer may pass. No member is discarded, and wounds, disability, bodily difference, or incomplete cure must not be narrated as curse-marks or moral disorder.
- He has no staff. His scarred hands, voice, movement, judgment, protection, and gathering must carry the action.
- The Shepherd may judge both the host and the flock; fleece cannot confer innocence.
- The circle gathers around him, but it cannot contain him or become the sole fold of salvation.
- The Shepherd's old wounds should point toward the cross without requiring a one-to-one allegory in every scene.

### C. Lordship without domination

#### Primary texts

- Philippians 2:1-11
- Mark 10:35-45
- John 13:1-17
- John 15:9-17

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

Philippians 2 places its Christological poem inside an appeal concerning communal rivalry, self-interest, humility, and concern for others. The movement descends through servanthood and obedience to death on a cross, then turns to God's exaltation and the confession of Jesus Christ as Lord.

Mark 10 directly contrasts rulers who dominate with greatness expressed as service. The saying about the Son of Man serving and giving his life follows a request for privileged seats, so it judges status-seeking within the disciple community.

John 13 locates footwashing within Jesus' knowledge that he comes from God and returns to God. Lordship is not denied before service; the one called Lord and Teacher performs the servant's act and gives it as a pattern.

John 15 joins remaining in Christ, keeping his command, mutual love, friendship rather than servile ignorance, chosen fruitfulness, and self-giving love. Creaturely remaining is explicitly dependent: branches do not produce life from themselves.

#### B2 - Disputed questions

- Philippians 2:6 can be read through preexistence Christology, Adam Christology, or a synthesis. `Harpagmos` is variously translated as something seized, grasped, retained, or exploited.
- `Ekenosen` ("emptied himself") does not specify an emptied substance. The following participles narrate taking servant form and human likeness; the text does not straightforwardly teach that Christ discarded divinity.
- C. A. Wanamaker documents the major divide between readings centered on preexistence and readings centered on an Adamic contrast. The project need not settle every aspect of that debate to retain the passage's high-confidence narrative movement and communal force.
- Mark 10:45's "ransom for many" has a large atonement-history beyond this dossier. It should not be reduced to a leadership slogan.

#### B3 - Canonical synthesis

Christ's authority is neither the refusal of lordship nor domination with benevolent intent. It is lordship enacted in self-giving service, cross-shaped obedience, and life-giving relation, followed by divine vindication rather than self-exaltation.

#### B4 - Constructive inference

"Keeping without possessing" can function as a Christ-shaped ethical analogy for created guardians, but it is not an exhaustive definition of Christ's work. Christ does not merely model non-possession; he reconciles, gives life, judges, and reigns in ways no guardian or myth can reproduce.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- The Shepherd cannot be reduced to gentleness, vulnerability, or refusal to act.
- The Shepherd must not seize a throne in order to defeat enthronement.
- Guardians may imitate service and relinquishment, but their suffering does not become redemptive capital.
- "Self-emptying" must never be used to demand that an abused or dependent creature remain available to harm.
- The Shepherd's action should create agency in others rather than cultivated dependence upon his dramatic rescue.

### D. Shepherd and Lamb without symbolic collapse

#### Primary texts

- John 1:29-36
- Revelation 5
- Revelation 7:9-17
- Revelation 13:11

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

Revelation 5 announces a conquering lion but presents a standing Lamb bearing signs of slaughter. The Lamb receives the scroll, is acclaimed with God, and is declared worthy because of his death and its ransoming effect. The image combines wound, life, authority, worship, and action.

Revelation 7 makes the symbolic convergence explicit: the Lamb at the throne's center shepherds the multitude and leads them to living water. Lamb and Shepherd are not competing offices in Christ. Revelation 13 adds a warning: lamb-like appearance can mask dragon-like speech and coercive power.

John 1's title "Lamb of God" has several proposed backgrounds, including Passover, Isaiah's servant, and Revelation-like victorious Lamb imagery. The text should not be forced into a single background where the canon itself develops multiple resonances.

#### B3 - Canonical synthesis

Christ as Lamb prevents Shepherd language from becoming distant or merely managerial sovereignty. Christ as Shepherd prevents Lamb language from identifying helplessness or victimhood with holiness. The same Christ is wounded, living, active, judging, gathering, and worshiped.

#### B4 - Constructive inference

The project's moral symmetry between fang and fleece is compatible with Ezekiel 34's judgment among sheep and Revelation 13's false lamb imagery. Yet ordinary lambs must not be treated as miniature Christs simply because they suffer.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- The individual lamb in *Aru Va'en* is a creature under the Shepherd's care, not an incarnation or sacrificial substitute.
- Its positive vocation should include truthful agency, response, movement, witness, or participation; passive suffering is insufficient.
- The Living Lamb may gather the flock as the kept-and-gathered face of the communion also seen from Aruvan's keeping side, but it must not absorb the flock into itself as a competing salvific center.
- The individual lamb remains itself and becomes the first visible participant in the Living Lamb during regathering without becoming Christ, ceasing to be creaturely, or undergoing metamorphosis.
- The Shepherd may bear both Shepherd and Lamb resonance, but the narration should not transfer that unique union to every fleece-bearing character.
- A lamb-like figure can be false; appearance cannot certify virtue.

### E. Cross, resurrection, wounds, and morning

#### Primary texts

- John 20:19-29
- 1 Corinthians 15
- Revelation 21:1-22:5
- Isaiah 25:6-9
- Luke 22:14-20, 28-30
- Revelation 19:6-9

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

John 20 identifies the risen Jesus through the wounds of crucifixion. The wounds are neither the whole meaning of resurrection nor erased as though the crucifixion never happened. The risen Jesus brings peace, sends the disciples, and gives the Spirit.

First Corinthians 15 insists on both resurrection continuity and transformation. Paul argues against reducing resurrection to non-bodily survival, while his seed and body analogies resist imagining resurrection as simple restoration of the prior condition. Christ's resurrection is firstfruits, and the final enemy is death.

Revelation 21 attributes new creation to God: death, mourning, crying, and pain end because God dwells with humanity and makes all things new. The scene is eschatological and symbolic; it should not be used to deny grief or promise immediate historical repair.

Isaiah 25 places a feast prepared by the Lord beside the swallowing up of death and the wiping away of tears. Luke 22 places Jesus' final meal with his disciples in expectation of fulfillment in the kingdom of God. Revelation 19 presents the marriage supper of the Lamb as eschatological celebration. These texts join shared food, divine provision, communion, judgment, and future fulfillment without making every meal the consummated Feast.

#### B3 - Canonical synthesis

Christian morning is not mere survival, recurrence, or a clean return to innocence. It is life through death, divine transformation, embodied recognition, remembered wounds, defeated death, restored communion, and new creation. Scripture's feast imagery gives that hope a bodily and communal grammar: creatures receive provision together while awaiting the final overcoming of death.

#### B4 - Constructive inference

The myth may use morning as resurrection-shaped hope without claiming that its characters undergo literal eschatological resurrection. If the scene remains earthly aftermath, dead companions cannot simply reappear restored. If it becomes a vision or eschatological compression, the narrative must signal that mode rather than slide into it for emotional repair. A shared meal can serve as a constructive foretaste of the Feast to come if Harrow's absence, unfinished reconciliation, and the distinction between creaturely food and Christ's saving work remain explicit.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- Morning must not erase Little Vey's body, the companions' wounds, culpability, grief, or material consequences.
- Retained wounds may witness continuity and truth, but shining scars should not romanticize injury.
- The Black Sun can be borne and overcome without becoming the necessary source of salvation.
- The Shepherd, not ordeal itself, opens morning.
- The final state must create living agency, not merely beautiful stillness.
- Let the meal follow counting the missing, tending the wounded, and covering the dead; celebration must not displace care or mourning.
- The returned may be fed without being declared innocent, restored to trust or office, or released from restitution and discernment.
- No creature, especially no lamb, should be killed for the meal. Eucharistic resonance must not become a new sacrament, repeated institution narrative, or rival saving act.
- Keep Harrow's absence perceptible at the meal so earthly bread points beyond itself rather than pretending that death has already been swallowed up.

## II. Troth and Christ

### Textual discipline

No biblical term simply means the project's full category of Troth. Several textual fields contribute legitimate controls:

- God's faithfulness and calling: 1 Corinthians 1:9; 2 Timothy 2:8-13.
- covenant love and fidelity: Exodus 34:6 and its echoes in John 1:14.
- truth personally disclosed in Christ: John 14:6-11.
- Christ as "Faithful and True" and Word of God: Revelation 19:11-16.
- remaining in Christ and bearing fruit: John 15:1-17.
- God's promises receiving their "Yes" in Christ: 2 Corinthians 1:18-22.

These passages do not authorize a claim that `Troth` is a biblical lexical category. They support a B3-B4 synthesis in which divine fidelity is personal, Christologically disclosed, covenantal, truth-bearing, and participatory.

### Recommended layered formulation

#### Divine level

The Troth does not exist as a reality external to God. It names, constructively, God's own unwavering fidelity and the right relation that flows from God's character and action.

#### Christological level

Jesus Christ is the definitive personal disclosure and enactment of that fidelity. His incarnation, ministry, cross, resurrection, judgment, mercy, and reign determine what Christians may mean by faithful relation. Christ does not answer to the Troth as a second authority.

#### Participatory level

Created persons and communities are called by the Spirit to participate in this fidelity. Their keeping remains partial, accountable, correctable, and dependent. They can betray the Troth precisely because they never possess it.

#### Liturgical level

"The Troth remains" confesses neither institutional survival nor human righteousness. It confesses that God's fidelity in Christ is not destroyed by creaturely failure and that truthful return remains possible.

### Required doctrinal correction

The following formulations should be deprecated in active doctrine:

- "The Shepherd is faithful to the Troth" when this implies an external standard over Christ.
- "The Troth provides the standard by which the Shepherd is judged."
- "The Shepherd's authority derives from his relation to the Troth."
- any sequence in which Troth is metaphysically first and Christ is its exemplary servant.

A corrected sequence is:

**Triune God revealed in Christ -> divine fidelity disclosed and enacted -> creaturely participation by the Spirit -> accountable forms of keeping, fellowship, office, myth, and liturgy.**

The trinitarian specificity of this sequence is accepted as constructive theology under D-073. It remains distinct from lexical exegesis and does not establish that every Trinitarian implication belongs explicitly in the mythic register. Its negative rule remains fixed: no second Logos and no independent Troth above Christ.

### Consequences for the definitive PDF

1. The opening line about the Troth and the following ache can remain only if the close interprets them: the Shepherd is personally prior, love is prior to grief, and the cry precedes the creaturely vow.
2. "The Troth in me remembered" should be treated as mythopoetic personification of formed conscience, covenantal memory, or creaturely participation, not a divine entity inside Vaerun.
3. "What remained after the Troth had passed through flesh" risks reversing incarnation. The final rewrite should instead suggest creatures judged, kept, or remade through the Shepherd's embodied fidelity.
4. The Shepherd's long silence must be revised into active keeping without making him a combatant.
5. The source's ending can retain its liturgical compression, but Vey must speak as a living embodied companion rather than a postmortem voice.

### Christological spine acceptance questions

These questions have been settled for the passage map. They remain controls for the final review of drafted prose:

1. **Settled by D-073:** The layered Troth formulation above is the accepted governing constructive synthesis.
2. **Settled by D-070:** "The opening ache" names the creaturely experience of broken relation and the cry that precedes a vow, rather than anything metaphysically prior to Christ. Preserve the PDF invocation exactly and let the Shepherd-prior and love-prior close supply its final Christological interpretation.
3. **Settled by D-018, D-032 through D-034, and D-062 through D-066:** The Shepherd's visible keeping combines active gathering, making room, truthful judgment, healing, and reception, while Christ remains the unique saving measure rather than one keeper among others.
4. **Settled by D-058 and D-059:** Morning is an ordinary earthly dawn on the same wounded hill and a genuine firstfruits of new creation in Christ, without becoming literal resurrection, a separate eschatological realm, or erased history. A shared meal embodies its foretaste of the Feast while remaining creaturely food rather than a new sacrament or completed reconciliation.

## III. Sin, deformation, accusation, and moral agency

### Governing research conclusion

The Black Sun may expose, accuse, deceive, and deform, but it must not become an infallible revealer or an alternate judge. Scripture permits specific evil to be named, rejects human innocence as a possession, and still refuses to make sin the whole or final identity of a person. The Shepherd must distinguish truthful exposure from totalizing accusation without pretending that every accusation is false.

### A. Universal sin without undifferentiated guilt

#### Primary texts

- Romans 2:1-16; 3:1-26.
- Ezekiel 18.
- Galatians 6:1-5.

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

- Romans 2 turns judgment back upon the one who judges and portrays conscience as alternately accusing and defending. Conscience is therefore morally significant but not self-validating; hidden matters remain subject to God's judgment through Christ.
- Romans 3 places Jews and Greeks alike under sin, excludes boasting, and locates justification in God's grace through Christ. The same argument insists that human unfaithfulness does not nullify God's faithfulness and rejects doing evil so that good may result.
- Ezekiel 18 rejects the transfer of a parent's guilt to a child, names concrete injustice, and insists that a wicked person may turn and live. Personal accountability and the possibility of return appear together.
- Galatians 6 joins gentle restoration to self-watch, mutual burden-bearing to personal responsibility, and correction to a warning against self-importance.

#### B2 - Literary and interpretive cautions

- Paul's argument in Romans 1-3 concerns Jew and Gentile within a larger account of God's impartial judgment and covenant fidelity. It should not be reduced to the slogan that all parties in every conflict are equally culpable.
- Ezekiel 18 addresses responsibility across generations in an exilic dispute. Its principle of individual accountability does not deny corporate consequences, structural evil, or the injuries that one person's acts impose on another.
- The conscience language of Romans 2 does not teach that inward certainty is revelation. Conscience may accuse or defend; Christ remains the judge of what is hidden.

#### B3 - Canonical synthesis

No person may certify personal righteousness, but neither universal sin nor humility abolishes moral distinctions among acts, agents, victims, bystanders, and consequences. Objective evil can be named. Culpability must be truthful and proportionate. A person's history matters, yet neither ancestry nor a past act constitutes an immutable essence.

#### B4 - Constructive inference

Unclaimed Virtue should produce a double refusal: no character may claim innocence as a possession, and no accuser may claim omniscience. The Troth category can name accountable relation only beneath Christ's judgment; it cannot authorize a community to turn its diagnosis into a final identity sentence.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- The Black Sun should work partly through recognizably true fragments: neglected cries, concealed fear, misused authority, abandoned companions, self-protective vows, and remembered harm.
- Its deformation lies in isolating those truths from mercy, relation, proportion, and possible return. It says, in effect, not merely "you did this" but "this is all you are and all you can be."
- The companions must confess or confront particular acts and failures. A generalized cloud of shame would weaken both agency and repair.
- The Shepherd must neither dismiss the accusations nor endorse their totalizing verdict. His keeping should disclose what is true, reject what is false, and reopen responsible action.
- Aruvan's accumulated keeping may be tested and corrected. Longevity cannot certify him.

#### B6 - Liturgical and pastoral constraint

Confession may name sin concretely, but the assembly must not perform public omniscience, demand self-annihilation, or declare a living person irredeemable. A refrain of unclaimed virtue should implicate the speakers before it licenses them to accuse another.

#### Misuse test

This synthesis fails if it is used to say that aggressor and injured person are equally guilty in a specific act, that the cry proves every interpretation attached to it, that repentance erases consequences, or that a keeper's discernment is immune from correction.

### B. The accuser and truthful testimony

#### Primary texts

- Revelation 12:7-12.
- Romans 2:12-16.
- Deuteronomy 19:15-21; Proverbs 18:13, 17.

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

- Revelation 12 depicts the dragon as deceiver and accuser, and the faithful overcome through the Lamb and their testimony. The chapter does not identify every accusation with falsehood; it identifies an accusatory power whose work opposes God's reign and the Lamb.
- Deuteronomy 19 requires corroboration for a judicial charge and subjects a malicious witness to inquiry. Proverbs warns both against answering before hearing and against accepting the first account without examination.

#### B3-B4 - Canonical and constructive inference

The cry is a morally urgent summons to hear, protect, and investigate. It is not an infallible oracle that settles every fact or remedy by itself. Testimony must not be silenced in the name of neutrality, and it must not be converted into unreviewable sovereignty. Hearing, corroboration, mercy, and judgment remain joined under Christ.

#### B5 - Mythic constraint

The Black Sun may counterfeit the form of testimony by repeating real words without relation or hope. The myth should therefore distinguish at least three things through action and image: the cry that summons care, the testimony that names what happened, and the accusatory verdict that forecloses truth-seeking and return.

## IV. Repentance, redemption, repair, and return

### Governing research conclusion

Biblical return is initiated and enabled by grace, embodied in changed action, and ordered toward restored relation. It is not measured by emotional intensity alone. Forgiveness, reconciliation, safety, restitution, restored trust, and restored office are related but non-identical goods; the myth must not collapse them into one dawn scene.

The user-established constructive formulation is: **salvation is allowing oneself to participate in the proper ordering of the Shepherd**. Within the biblical controls above, `allowing oneself` names yielded, non-coerced participation in grace, not the creature's production of salvation. The Shepherd seeks, calls, judges, heals, and opens room first. His ordering is right relation beneath his lordship, not compliance with a guardian circle, office, or institution.

### A. Being sought and turning toward home

#### Primary texts

- Ezekiel 18:21-32.
- Luke 15.
- Romans 5:6-11; 8:1-4.

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

- Ezekiel couples a real turn from injustice with God's declared desire that the wicked turn and live.
- Luke 15 presents divine initiative and joy through the shepherd and woman who seek, and through the father who sees and runs. The younger son's return includes confession; the elder son's alienation remains unresolved at the end of the parable.
- Romans 5 locates reconciliation in God's action toward enemies through Christ. Romans 8's "no condemnation" belongs to those in Christ and does not mean that sin is unreal; the passage says that God condemned sin in the flesh.

#### B2-B3 - Interpretive and canonical synthesis

Luke 15 should not be flattened into one mechanism: the sheep and coin are found, the younger son returns and confesses, and the elder son is entreated without a narrated resolution. Together the stories hold divine initiative, human response, joy, and unresolved refusal without making one detail carry every account of repentance.

#### B4 - Constructive inference

Return remains possible because God's fidelity in Christ precedes and exceeds the failure, not because failure is negligible. The returned person does not possess redemption, demand trust, or prove righteousness by completing a ritual. Return restores agency under mercy and truth.

The Shepherd's proper ordering is participatory rather than coercive: a creature may cease resistance and enter the relation already opened for it. Such yielding is a real creaturely act, yet it answers prior grace and therefore cannot be narrated as self-rescue. Participation begins salvation's lived reordering without implying that confession, restitution, repaired trust, or restored responsibility is already complete.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- The Shepherd should make return possible before any fallen character can earn it.
- A fallen character must still turn, answer, relinquish deception, or begin repair; being sought does not erase agency.
- The returning sheep should recognize the wolves as a sign that its relations are returning to their proper order beneath the Shepherd. This recognition must point through the wolves to the Shepherd rather than make guardians the source, judge, or necessary mediator of salvation.
- The final gathering may include different distances from restored communion. Presence at morning need not imply equal trust, unchanged role, or completed repair.
- Little Vey's survival and voice prevent reconciliation from being narrated entirely around the wrongdoer's relief.

### B. Fruit, restitution, and responsible sorrow

#### Primary texts

- Luke 19:1-10.
- 2 Corinthians 7:8-13.
- Galatians 6:1-5.

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

- Jesus' welcome of Zacchaeus precedes the narrated declaration of restitution, while Zacchaeus names concrete economic repair and repayment. The episode joins welcome, salvation, and material consequence.
- In 2 Corinthians 7, grief is not itself repentance. Paul distinguishes a grief oriented toward God by the earnest response it produces from a grief that produces death.
- Galatians 6 requires restoration in gentleness, vigilance against one's own temptation, shared burdens, and personal responsibility.

#### B2 - Context and caution

Paul's account in 2 Corinthians concerns a particular conflict whose reconstruction remains disputed, and the letter includes Paul's claim that the community demonstrated innocence in the matter. The passage cannot supply a universal checklist by which leaders force visible distress or rapid compliance from an accused person.

#### B3-B4 - Canonical and constructive inference

Repentance is judged by truthful reorientation and fitting fruit, not by tears, humiliation, or eloquence. Restitution cannot undo all harm, but where repair is possible it belongs to return. Grace may precede repair without rendering repair optional.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- Tears, wounds, kneeling, or speaking a vow cannot function as automatic proof of repentance.
- At least one consequential act should show a turn: releasing a claim, naming a concealed truth, returning what was taken, accepting a boundary, protecting another at cost, or undertaking unfinished repair.
- The morning may begin repair; it must not falsely depict every consequence as completed before the myth ends.

### C. Correction, forgiveness, reconciliation, and boundaries

#### Primary texts

- Matthew 18:1-35.
- Galatians 6:1-5.

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

- Matthew 18 frames correction and forgiveness with humility, concern for "little ones," severe warnings against causing them to stumble, and the seeking of the strayed sheep.
- Matthew 18:15-20 provides an escalating communal process that aims to "gain" the other and can end in exclusion. Verses 21-35 immediately intensify the demand for forgiveness.
- The phrase "against you" in Matthew 18:15 has a significant manuscript variant. The passage should not be made to support a complete institutional procedure on the basis of that phrase alone.

#### Rival readings and pastoral warning

Some readings stress reinstatement of the straying member; others stress the protection of vulnerable members and the power asymmetries that church procedures can conceal. Elaine Ramshaw argues that reading the chapter piecemeal can let discipline serve authorities and let repeated forgiveness expose injured people to further abuse. Ksenija Magda reads Matthew 18-19 with Mark 9-10 and centers harm to "little ones" and abuses of power. Magda's sharper claim that human conflicts always divide cleanly into one perpetrator and one victim should not be universalized; it is a needed corrective to false equivalence, not a replacement for factual discernment.

#### B3-B4 - Canonical and constructive inference

Forgiveness refuses vengeance and the final possession of a debt. Reconciliation is the truthful rebuilding of relation and may require repentance, time, safety, and repair. Trust is a prudential judgment. Office is an entrusted responsibility with qualifications. None of these can be inferred automatically from another.

#### B5-B6 - Mythic and liturgical constraints

- A harmed character may forgive without being required to resume former proximity or vulnerability.
- A fallen keeper may be received as a person without immediately being restored to a former task.
- Communal speech must not pressure the harmed to pronounce closure for the sake of a beautiful ending.
- The closing refrain may promise companionship in unfinished repair: "we do not keep alone" must include witnesses, boundaries, and shared responsibility, not merely emotional reunion.

## V. Violence, non-retaliation, and peaceful resistance

### Governing research conclusion

The active revision should reject violence as redemptive resolution and portray strong peaceful resistance to physical, spiritual, and institutional assault. Scripture clearly forbids personal vengeance and commands enemy-love; interpreters disagree about whether Matthew 5 encodes specific tactics of active nonviolent resistance, a more radical nonresistance, rhetorical intensification, or a distinction between personal response and public protection. The myth may construct active peaceful resistance, but it must label that move B4-B5 rather than claiming it as the uncontested lexical meaning of Matthew 5.

### A. Matthew 5 and the disputed shape of resistance

#### Primary texts

- Matthew 5:38-48.
- Luke 6:27-36.

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

- The lex talionis limited retaliation within Israel's legal tradition; Jesus addresses his disciples' response to an evildoer and gives concrete commands involving insult, legal seizure, compelled service, begging, and enemy-love.
- The immediate movement is away from reciprocal harm and toward action that seeks the enemy's good. The text does not say that evil is good, that danger is unreal, or that a harmed person must remain physically available to repeated assault.

#### Rival readings

- Walter Wink's influential reading understands the cheek, cloak, and second-mile sayings as creative actions that expose domination while refusing violence and passivity.
- Kevin Carnahan disputes the claim that these sayings encode the historical Jesus' program of unarmed direct action and reads the surrounding imperatives as more radical relinquishment.
- Dion Forster judges Wink's detailed historical reconstruction exegetically contestable while retaining active nonviolent resistance as a defensible constructive and ethical appropriation.
- Other interpreters distinguish an individual's refusal of retaliation from protective or public responsibilities. That distinction may be pastorally important, but Matthew 5 itself does not present a developed theory of offices or state force.

#### B3-B4 - Canonical and constructive inference

The firm canonical center is non-retaliation, enemy-love, truthfulness, and refusal to become what one resists. Active peaceful resistance is a warranted constructive direction when joined to Jesus' protection of the vulnerable, exposure of hypocrisy, refusal of coercive kingship, and command to overcome evil with good. Its precise tactics are not a settled B1 claim.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- No blow, weapon, death, or self-destruction may break the Black Sun or morally resolve the conflict.
- Resistance should be visible and consequential: making room for escape, interposing, holding, carrying, refusing a false command, naming the concealed act, gathering the isolated, or accepting cost without transferring that cost to the vulnerable.
- Use the least-harm ladder established by D-030: hold before hitting; if holding fails, throw an immediate attacker back with controlled bodily force; do not use fang or claw to draw blood; never maim or kill.
- The resisting wolves may bleed. When they falter in restraint, the Shepherd's grief should visibly deepen as truthful exposure, not magical punishment or emotional coercion.
- Enemy-love must preserve the opponent's creaturely humanity and possibility of return; it does not require entrusting the opponent with access, command, or an unprotected victim.
- D-039 fixes the narrative grammar: a permeable circle opens inward for wounded and returning persons; wolves use bodies, shoulders, paired containment, and non-puncturing holds; every controlled throw restores space and ends at the boundary. Vaerun first falters by beginning pursuit, another wolf holds him, the Shepherd's grief grows, and Vaerun returns. The action remains costly because the wolves bleed, tire, and lose ground.

### B. Vengeance, public authority, and beastly power

#### Primary texts

- Romans 12:9-13:7.
- Acts 5:27-32.
- Revelation 13.

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

- Romans 12 forbids repayment of evil for evil and personal vengeance, commands blessing of persecutors and concrete care of enemies, and culminates in overcoming evil with good.
- Romans 13 describes governing authority as ordered toward good and as an agent of wrath against wrongdoing. The passage follows the prohibition of personal vengeance, but interpreters differ over how directly the two paragraphs allocate vengeance between believers and the state.
- Acts 5 depicts religious authorities issuing a direct prohibition that the apostles refuse because obedience to God takes precedence; the refusal centers witness to the crucified and risen Jesus.
- Revelation 13 depicts political and religious authority becoming idolatrous, coercive, deceptive, economically controlling, and murderous. The dragon can give "authority"; possession of power therefore cannot certify righteousness.

#### B2-B3 - Historical and canonical tension

David Horrell reads Romans 12-15 as envisioning a nonconformist, nonviolent Christian community while Romans 13 sanctions state force, leaving a genuine ethical dilemma for Christians who also act as citizens. Romans 13 cannot be erased, but Acts 5 and Revelation 13 prevent its conversion into unlimited obedience or the axiom that whatever an authority does is righteous. The canon preserves a tension requiring discernment rather than a single timeless institutional formula.

#### B4 - Constructive inference

Authority assigns responsibility to seek good, restrain wrong, and accept stricter judgment; it does not transfer divine moral certainty to the officeholder. Peaceful resistance may include disobedience to an evil command and the loss of office, safety, or standing. The myth need not solve Christian theories of police, war, or state force, and should not imply that its symbolic resolution has done so.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- The Black Sun may imitate legitimate authority, demand assent, and punish noncompliance; its power must not be treated as evidence that it bears the Troth.
- Hrovan, Vaerun, Vaelun, elder, keeper, and assembly remain accountable to the good they are responsible to serve.
- A character may relinquish authority rather than carry out a destructive command. Another may resist that command without thereby claiming personal righteousness.

### C. Suffering without sanctifying abuse

#### Primary texts

- 1 Peter 2:18-25; 3:8-17.
- Luke 22:35-53; John 18:1-11.

#### B1-B2 - Text and context

- First Peter directly addresses enslaved household members, including those suffering under unjust masters, and places Christ's non-retaliatory suffering before them. The text does not abolish the slave system or address masters in the corresponding section.
- At Jesus' arrest, a disciple uses a sword, Jesus halts the violence, and Luke uniquely narrates the healing of the injured ear. The preceding "two swords" exchange has competing interpretations; Luke's narrator says the disciples misunderstand Jesus' figurative warning, while the arrest scene does not provide a general policy for every protective act.

#### Historical-critical and pastoral warning

Chris L. de Wet argues that real institutional slavery is central, not incidental, to 1 Peter's construction of suffering Christian identity. That makes the passage morally difficult and sharply limits any direct transfer to people who can escape abuse or to leaders instructing subordinates. The text can witness to Christ's presence with the unjustly harmed; it must never be used to command an abused person to stay accessible to violence, preserve an institution's reputation, or treat injury as spiritually desirable.

#### B3-B5 - Canonical synthesis and mythic constraints

Christ's passion is saving in a unique sense; creaturely suffering is not redemptive currency. Faithful endurance may refuse retaliation and bear witness when harm cannot yet be escaped, but preventable suffering should not be manufactured or assigned to others. In the myth, the Shepherd bears cost while actively stopping assault and progressively healing the lamb; ordinary characters must not be praised merely for remaining breakable. Healing does not erase injury but deepens the Shepherd's grief, which strengthens the circle as room and steadiness rather than violence.

#### Misuse test for Part V

This synthesis fails if "peace" means the aggressor's undisturbed control, if protection becomes revenge, if the harmed are required to absorb violence for others, if office makes coercion holy, or if the Black Sun is defeated by the same logic of domination it embodies.

## VI. Communal keeping, differentiated callings, authority, and the cry

### Governing research conclusion

The New Testament joins one Lord and one Spirit to diverse gifts, tasks, and degrees of responsibility. Diversity is not a ladder of moral worth, but neither is it the denial of office, competence, or accountable decision. The community hears the cry, tests testimony, protects those exposed to harm, and remains correctable because no member or institution is the whole body and no office is Christ.

### A. One body, different callings

#### Primary texts

- 1 Corinthians 12-13.
- Romans 12:3-8.
- James 3:1-18.

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

- First Corinthians 12 locates diverse gifts in the same Spirit, forms of service in the same Lord, and workings in the same God. Every manifestation is given for common benefit.
- The body image denies self-exclusion and self-sufficiency, gives special honor to members treated as weaker, and requires shared suffering and joy.
- Paul still names differentiated functions and gives apostles a kind of priority. The body metaphor therefore cannot prove that every role has identical responsibility or competence.
- Romans 12 joins differentiated gifts to sober self-assessment. James 3 subjects teachers to stricter judgment and contrasts selfish ambition with wisdom that is peaceable, gentle, merciful, fruitful, and sincere.

#### B3-B4 - Canonical and constructive inference

Vaelun, Vaerun, and Hrovan can be different callings without becoming ascending grades of holiness. Office identifies a task, scope, and burden of accountability. Competence may justify entrusting a decision; neither competence nor office proves that the decision is righteous. Greater influence creates greater exposure to judgment and correction.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- Remove every narrative cue that treats the callings as stages of ascent or Hrovan as the spiritually completed form of Vaerun.
- Do not foreshadow Vaerun becoming Hrovan. His later letters arise from his own calling and fallibility.
- Hrovan's distinctive action should fit his calling without making him the Shepherd's successor, representative incarnation, or highest moral species.
- Little Vey must retain consequential perception and speech. Vulnerability is not the same as passivity, and age or weakness does not make a character an oracle.

### B. Deliberation, oversight, and correction

#### Primary texts

- Acts 15:1-35.
- 1 Peter 5:1-5.
- Acts 5:27-32.

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

- Acts 15 portrays open dispute, testimony from mission, appeal to Scripture, a judgment proposed by James, agreement involving the whole church, delegated messengers, and a written decision attributed to the Holy Spirit and the assembly.
- Acts 15 is not frictionless consensus: "much debate" precedes the decision, and Paul and Barnabas later separate after a sharp disagreement.
- The relationship between Acts 15 and Paul's account in Galatians 2 is historically disputed. Acts should not be treated as a complete constitutional transcript or a guarantee that an assembly using similar steps cannot err.
- First Peter 5 charges elders to tend rather than dominate, to be examples, and to answer to the chief Shepherd. Acts 5 shows that religious office can issue a command that faithful witnesses must refuse.

#### B3-B4 - Canonical and constructive inference

Healthy communal discernment requires truthful disagreement, relevant witnesses, Scriptural judgment, attention to the Spirit, a responsible decision, transparent communication, and ongoing corrigibility. No procedure transforms a provisional human judgment into the Troth. An entrusted leader may need to decide; the community may need to challenge, appeal, limit, or remove that leader.

#### B5-B6 - Mythic and liturgical constraints

- "We do not keep alone" should mean distributed witness and correction, not collective possession of righteousness.
- The myth may portray someone deciding under responsibility, but should also show that the decision is witnessed, answerable, and costly.
- Any gathered response should remain a provisional fellowship rather than the founding of a canonical capital-O Order.
- A liturgical assembly may confess, listen, and commission; it must not identify itself, its offices, or its survival with the Troth.

### C. Hearing the cry without making it sovereign

#### Primary texts

- Exodus 2:23-25; 3:7-10.
- Isaiah 58.
- Luke 10:25-37.
- Matthew 25:31-46.
- Deuteronomy 19:15-21; Proverbs 18:13, 17.

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

- In Exodus, God hears Israel's groaning, remembers the covenant, sees, knows, and sends Moses toward liberation. Hearing issues in divine attention and action, not sentiment alone.
- Isaiah 58 rejects worship severed from release of oppression, food, shelter, clothing, and responsibility for one's kin.
- Luke 10 shifts the question from defining who qualifies as neighbor to acting as a neighbor through concrete mercy across a hostile social boundary.
- Matthew 25 identifies treatment of "the least of these my brothers" with treatment of the Son of Man. ### Vethra as costly first light: claim-class review

#### Primary texts

- Romans 8:18-25
- 2 Corinthians 4:6-18
- Colossians 3:1-4
- 1 John 3:1-3
- Revelation 21:22-27

#### B1 - Textual and literary claims

Romans 8 joins the firstfruits of the Spirit to present groaning and patient hope for the redemption of the body. Firstfruits are therefore real present participation without completed transformation or escape from mortality.

Second Corinthians 4 speaks of God-given light in human hearts and treasure carried in earthen vessels. The passage holds weakness, affliction, costly apostolic witness, inward renewal, and coming glory together. It does not make suffering intrinsically holy or establish pain as payment for glory.

Colossians 3 describes present life as hidden with Christ and future appearance in glory as dependent upon Christ's appearing. First John 3 similarly distinguishes present identity as God's children from what has not yet appeared and grounds future likeness in seeing Christ as he is. Revelation 21 reserves the final city's light to the glory of God, with the Lamb as its lamp.

#### B3 - Canonical synthesis

Christian firstfruits are received from God in Christ and by the Spirit while creatures remain mortal, vulnerable, correctable, and unfinished. Present light anticipates rather than generates consummation. Costly fidelity may bear witness to this hope, but the fullness comes from Christ and includes bodily redemption that creaturely endurance cannot purchase.

#### B4 - Constructive inference

Vethra may mythopoetically image hidden first light beneath the ash of mortality: a creaturely beginning of eschatological relation in Christ. This is a project-specific synthesis, not the lexical meaning of `firstfruits`, `light`, `glory`, or any biblical term. The PDF's `coal-under-ash` for Vaelun and `last light under ash` for Vey provide the myth's internal vocabulary; D-060 extends that imagery to Vethra without claiming the names are synonymous.

Costliness belongs to Vethra because participation is embodied through restraint, remaining, relinquishment, grief, correction, responsibility, and vulnerability. Cost is neither meritorious payment nor a measurable quantity of pain. An unwounded companion can bear costly Vethra, while a severe wound or death cannot certify fidelity.

#### B5 - Mythic constraints

- Vethra must remain subtle first light, not visible halos or uniform luminescence.
- The Shepherd remains its source and measure; the Troth does not become an autonomous flame or second Logos.
- Establish Vethra across the whole company through the accepted communal frame, including wounded and unwounded bearers, then pause only over Harrow and Ostra. Murn and Keld remain bearers without separate Morning portraits.
- Harrow's dead flesh bears Vethra without animation, incorruption, operative presence, moral certification, or resurrection before the eschaton.
- Harrow's death purchases nothing. His Vethra points beyond earthly Morning without making death useful or holy.
- Later failure can obscure a living bearer's Vethra; it does not remove the need for judgment, repentance, or correction.

### Recommended return sequence

The first return should combine recognition, refusal, and accountable action rather than relying on a word or emotion alone:

1. The Shepherd works within the circle and calls his flock continually throughout the struggle; one lost sheep hears him within a near-likeness, begins to remember, and answers with a cry too soft for the other wolves.
2. Little Vey hears that answer and smells remembrance beginning within the sheep. He does not announce, interpret, or name what he perceives; out of faithfulness to Vaerun and trust in his calling, he seeks him. This alerts Vaerun first, and Vaerun's changed attention then alerts the others.
3. The lost sheep moves ever so subtly toward the Shepherd. This is the first visible act of orientation, not completed repentance or proof of innocence.
4. The sheep stops participating in the assault, refuses the next impulse or command to harm, and opens a way for a smaller or injured creature.
5. The bounded circle recognizes the turn and opens. The smaller or injured creature passes first, and the returning sheep follows without any fang closing upon either. The sheep bears the sealed-eye deformation. The Shepherd draws away its ash- or wax-like film without removing the eye, restoring perception rather than discarding the member.
6. The first thing the sheep sees clearly is the wolves holding the passage without biting. It recognizes their restrained keeping as a sign that it is returning to its own correctly ordered relation beneath the Shepherd, then looks to the Shepherd for whom they hold the passage. This received and reordered body makes room visible to Host and flock. Some return, some withdraw, and some continue the assault; morning does not universalize the outcome.
7. Confession begins in these actions. Any words come later and remain brief; restitution, boundaries, repaired trust, and changed practice remain unfinished work after the Keeping.

This sequence enacts the user-established formulation that salvation is allowing oneself to participate in the proper ordering of the Shepherd. It lets Vey's vocation matter without making him interpreter, savior, or hidden engine of the scene; lets a fallen person yield and act without self-redemption; and makes the Shepherd's prior and continuing call the beginning of return and the condition of the opened circle. The Shepherd's opening rather than removal of the curse-sealed eye enacts separation without severing and keeps curse-deformation distinct from bodily difference or disability. The sheep's first clear sight of the wolves signals relational reordering, but the sign points through the wolves to the Shepherd and does not subordinate salvation to guardianship. Vey's seeking of Vaerun communicates his perception through relation and movement rather than explanation. His trust is a witness of faithfulness to a fallible companion, not a declaration that Vaerun is the measure. The wolves' protective work at the circumference mirrors the Shepherd's gathering, healing, and room-making within; it does not supply what his keeping lacks.

No one should place a passive lamb into Vaerun's jaws. ### Christological review

1. **Christ rather than Troth is the personal measure.** The candidate preserves the old invocation but never makes the Troth speak, heal, save, command, create, or judge as an independent agent. The final sequence places the Shepherd before the Troth and love before grief. This coheres with D-073's accepted constructive formulation and with the canonical priority of the Son reviewed in Part I.
2. **The Shepherd is recognizable without being announced.** His wounded human body, scarred palms, knowing call, non-possessive gathering, truthful judgment, reception of return, and meal-ordering carry the identification. Vey's final line names priority rather than revealing a concealed identity.
3. **The Shepherd and hill-lamb remain distinct.** The lamb is wounded, healed, remembers creaturely identity, chooses movement, accompanies a returning sheep, eats with the flock, and rests against the Shepherd. It does not atone, judge, command, die for others, become Christ, or absorb the flock. Christ's unique Shepherd-and-Lamb identity remains canonical control rather than a role transferred to the creaturely lamb.
4. **Healing is grace before response.** The Shepherd begins care while the lamb is helpless; the lamb's attention grows within prior care and does not purchase healing. The candidate does not claim that unhealed bodies are spiritually disordered or that bodily wholeness proves righteousness.
5. **The cross is not multiplied.** Harrow's death, the lamb's wounds, the wolves' blood, Vethra, and the Hrovan's confession purchase nothing. No creaturely suffering compels divine action or becomes a second saving sacrifice.

**Christological result:** **Pass in the final cross-Keeping audit.** The Aruvan/Living Lamb and Vethra passages carry substantial constructive theology, but the accepted D-085 compression lets image and action bear the distinctions that prevent symbolic collapse. The result is accepted under D-089 and the complete myth under D-090.

### Moral agency, accusation, and return

- The candidate uses action-conditioned language: one who attacks is an assailant in that action; one suffering is afflicted; one fallen remains capable of cessation, renewed harm, confession, and return. Body and shape never establish a fixed moral species.
- The Black Sun discloses actual failures, including Vaerun's pursuit and desire for silence. Its lie is the conversion of a true fragment into an exhaustive identity and foreclosed future. The Shepherd does not deny the fragment; his grief and action reopen truth-bearing responsibility.
- The returning sheep first hears the Shepherd, moves slightly toward him, refuses a command to harm, protects a smaller creature, enters through an opened boundary, receives restored sight, and looks through the wolves' restraint to the Shepherd. These acts begin confession but do not establish innocence, restitution, restored trust, or restored office.
- The smaller creature passes first. This prevents the scene from centering the wrongdoer's relief over the vulnerable creature's safety.
- Morning contains different distances from the Shepherd, and some do not return. Shared food does not force proximity, trust, or closure.

**Moral-agency result:** **Pass.** This remains a constructive salvation grammar. It must not be presented as the lexical meaning of Luke 15, John 10, repentance vocabulary, or reconciliation terminology.

### Violence and peaceful-resistance review

The candidate removes blood-drawing violence by the wolves as the resolving mechanism while preserving bodily danger, wounds, infection, exhaustion, loss of ground, and Harrow's death. Wolves interpose, carry, pin, rotate the wounded inward, hold fur without puncture, and throw only when holding fails. Vaerun's pursuit is interrupted by Thorn and judged by the Shepherd's grief. The Black Sun is not defeated by a blow, death, heroic endurance, or destruction of enemies.

Rival readings remain necessary:

- Matthew 5:38-42 has been read as absolute nonresistance, strategic nonviolent resistance, renunciation of retaliation, and instruction shaped by concrete first-century humiliations. The candidate's least-harm choreography is a project judgment under that canonical pressure, not the undisputed historical meaning of Jesus' examples.
- Romans 12 and 13 create a longstanding tension between ecclesial nonvengeance and public coercive authority. The myth depicts a bounded guardian company protecting immediate bodies; it does not settle Christian ethics of state force, policing, war, or every case of defense.
- First Peter's suffering language has been used pastorally to sanctify abuse. The candidate therefore never makes remaining under violence inherently holy, never demands access for an assailant, and never makes Harrow's death productive.

**Violence result:** **Pass as a bounded mythopoetic construction.** It must not be generalized into a complete casuistry of force without additional ethical work.
