# The Grammar of Apostasy Constitution

Version: 0.1.0
Status: Draft Canon
Repository: The Architecture of Apostasy
Audience: contributors, editors, reviewers, and AI agents
Purpose: canonical operating system for concept formation, research, writing, validation, and expansion

## 1. Preamble

The Architecture of Apostasy is a theological archive and writing project concerned with the recurring deformation of holy gifts into systems of idolatry, self-justification, institutional pride, embodied disorder, political worship, and false sacrifice. The Grammar of Apostasy is the internal constitution that governs how the archive speaks, reasons, defines terms, produces chapters, tests claims, and preserves conceptual unity across multiple authors, collaborators, and AI agents.

This constitution exists because the project is not only a collection of essays. It is a theological architecture. New chapters must be able to enter the archive without changing the meaning of the core terms, flattening the Christological center, or turning speculative synthesis into unmarked doctrine. Contributors may write in different voices, but they must write from the same grammar.

This document is intended to grow toward a full 150-250 page specification. Version 0.1.0 establishes the canonical structure, core definitions, research method, agent workflow, schema contracts, validation rules, and roadmap. Future versions should expand each section with examples, citations, diagrams, sample chapters, and adjudicated debates.

## 2. Constitutional Authority

This constitution has authority over internal project development. It does not claim ecclesial authority, confessional finality, or doctrinal infallibility. It is a rule of work for this repository.

The constitution governs:

1. Canonical terminology.
2. Theological method.
3. Research and citation standards.
4. Chapter architecture.
5. AI agent behavior.
6. Diagram conventions.
7. Knowledge graph structure.
8. Validation checklists.
9. Versioning and change control.
10. Repository architecture.
11. Continuous integration checks for consistency.

The constitution does not govern:

1. The conscience of contributors.
2. Ecclesial dogma outside the repository.
3. Private theological speculation not submitted for inclusion.
4. Public claims that have not passed project review.

## 3. The Central Thesis

God orders creation through the Logos. Created goods are entrusted to human and spiritual governance so that creation may be received, cultivated, and offered back to God in love. Fallen powers become self-referential, seek worship, and proclaim false symbolic orders. These false logoi are embodied through liturgies, habits, institutions, technologies, political forms, economies, and rituals of sacrifice. The human person becomes a chemical temple formed by these liturgies. Institutions and civilizations repeat the same pattern at scale. Christ exposes, judges, disarms, and restores the system through the cross, resurrection, Spirit, and Church.

The project studies this pattern across Scripture, church history, politics, war, addiction, technology, consumer culture, sacred time, religious institutions, and embodied formation.

## 5. Formal Ontology

The ontology is the governing conceptual map. Terms may be refined by versioned amendment, but contributors must not redefine them casually inside chapters.

### 5.1 God

God is the triune Creator, source, judge, and restorer of all created order. God is not one actor within the system but the one from whom all created being, meaning, vocation, and judgment derive.

Canonical function:

1. Ground of creation.
2. Source of the Logos.
3. Judge of false worship.
4. Giver of mercy.
5. Final end of restoration.

### 5.2 Logos

The Logos is God's ordering Word through whom creation coheres, by whom false orders are judged, and in whom all things are restored. In explicitly Christian usage, the Logos is finally identified with Jesus Christ.

Canonical function:

1. Divine order.
2. Meaning and intelligibility.
3. True speech against false speech.
4. Christological center.

Required distinction:

Use Logos for God's true ordering Word. Use disease logos, false logos, or counterfeit logos for fallen symbolic orders.

### 5.3 Creation

Creation is the good order made by God. It includes material, spiritual, personal, communal, institutional, ecological, symbolic, and historical realities. Creation is not evil in itself; it is subject to distortion, bondage, and restoration.

Canonical function:

1. The good gift under analysis.
2. The field in which vocation is received.
3. The object of distortion by fallen powers.
4. The object of restoration in Christ.

### 5.4 Created Good

A created good is any reality, capacity, structure, symbol, or institution that is good by origin and vocation before it is distorted by sin.

Examples:

1. Law.
2. Temple.
3. Family.
4. Nation.
5. Technology.
6. Desire.
7. Sacrifice.
8. Memory.
9. Political order.
10. Sacred time.

Analytical rule:

Every chapter must identify the created good before describing its corruption.

### 5.5 Principalities

Principalities are created governing powers intended to order domains of creation toward God. They may be discussed as spiritual powers, institutional powers, symbolic powers, or a complex participation of these dimensions, depending on the evidence and genre of the chapter.

Canonical function:

1. Governance of a domain.
2. Mediation of order.
3. Possible fall into self-reference.
4. Generator of false symbolic order when fallen.

Methodological restraint:

Do not collapse principalities into merely human institutions. Do not describe every social force as a principality without analysis.

### 5.6 Fall of the Powers

The fall of the powers is the movement by which a created governing authority becomes self-referential, refuses service to God, demands ultimate loyalty, and reorganizes its domain around its own preservation.

Diagnostic markers:

1. Self-reference.
2. Worship demand.
3. Sacrificial demand.
4. Immunity from critique.
5. Capture of symbols.
6. Rival eschatology.
7. Resistance to Christological exposure.

### 5.7 Disease Logos

Disease Logos names the false symbolic narrative proclaimed by fallen powers. It is not merely bad information. It is a formative order of meaning that explains the world, assigns guilt, offers salvation, identifies enemies, demands sacrifice, and trains desire.

Canonical function:

1. Counterfeit meaning.
2. False diagnosis.
3. False promise.
4. False anthropology.
5. False soteriology.
6. Script for fallen liturgy.

Required use:

Capitalize Disease Logos when referring to the project concept. Use lowercase when speaking generically of a false logos unless the chapter has defined a named instance.

### 5.8 Fallen Liturgy

A fallen liturgy is an embodied pattern of ritual, habit, symbol, speech, institution, or technology that incarnates a Disease Logos and forms persons or communities into its order.

Examples:

1. War ritual.
2. Political rally as worship.
3. Consumer unboxing as sacrament of acquisition.
4. Algorithmic attention capture.
5. Therapeutic self-justification.
6. Religious performance detached from repentance.

Canonical function:

1. Embodiment of false speech.
2. Training of attention.
3. Formation of desire.
4. Production of sacrifice.

### 5.9 Chemical Temple

The Chemical Temple is the embodied human person as formed through memory, attention, desire, neurochemistry, habit, trauma, emotion, imagination, and worship. It is not a reduction of personhood to chemistry. It is a way of naming the body as a living temple formed by liturgy.

Canonical function:

1. Site of embodied formation.
2. Bridge between theology and psychology.
3. Witness to the bodily consequences of worship.
4. Arena of restoration.

Guardrail:

Do not turn the Chemical Temple into medical determinism. Do not offer medical advice unless the chapter is explicitly qualified and reviewed.

### 5.10 Sacrifice

Sacrifice is the human impulse toward self-offering. It is good when ordered toward God in love and distorted when redirected toward idols, institutions, ideologies, enemies, or self-made righteousness.

Canonical function:

1. Reveals worship.
2. Reveals the object of ultimate loyalty.
3. Exposes the demand of the fallen power.
4. Prepares Christological resolution.

### 5.11 Scapegoat

The scapegoat is the person or group onto whom a fallen system transfers guilt, anxiety, pollution, or disorder so that the system can preserve itself without repentance.

Canonical function:

1. Exposes false peace.
2. Reveals sacrificial violence.
3. Connects political, religious, and social systems.
4. Prepares comparison with Christ, the innocent victim who exposes and ends false sacrifice.

### 5.12 Apostasy

Apostasy is the repeated transformation of holy gifts, symbols, institutions, rituals, and created goods into systems of pride, control, performance, idolatry, and self-justification.

Canonical pattern:

1. Gift.
2. Reception.
3. Institutionalization.
4. Externalization.
5. Idolatrous capture.
6. Prophetic exposure.
7. Judgment or pruning.
8. Christological restoration.

### 5.13 Nehushtan

Nehushtan is the project shorthand for a holy instrument detached from the living God and treated as a controllable object of power. The term is drawn from the bronze serpent narrative and its later destruction.

Canonical function:

1. Names the capture of holy symbols.
2. Distinguishes sign from source.
3. Diagnoses ritual without repentance.
4. Connects Israel, church history, and modern institutions.

### 5.14 Restoration

Restoration is the work of Christ by which false powers are exposed, false sacrifice is judged, created goods are returned to their proper end, and persons are re-formed by the Holy Spirit in the communion of the Church.

Canonical function:

1. Prevents critique from ending in despair.
2. Establishes the Christological center.
3. Distinguishes Christian judgment from cynicism.
4. Locates healing in grace rather than performance.

## 6. Formal Theological Methodology

Every major chapter should use the canonical method unless a deviation is explicitly justified.

### 6.1 Method Summary

1. Begin with Scripture.
2. Identify the created good.
3. Identify the governing principality or power.
4. Distinguish biblical teaching from historical theology and synthesis.
5. Trace the fall of the power.
6. Name the Disease Logos.
7. Identify the fallen liturgies.
8. Describe formation of the Chemical Temple.
9. Identify the sacrificial system.
10. Test the claim through history, anthropology, psychology, and systems analysis.
11. Surface objections and rival readings.
12. Resolve in Christ.

### 6.2 Order of Authority

The project uses the following order of authority:

1. Scripture.
2. Classical Christian doctrinal boundaries.
3. Historical theology.
4. Primary historical sources.
5. Philosophy and anthropology.
6. Psychology and embodied formation research.
7. Systems mapping.
8. Speculative synthesis.

Claims lower in the hierarchy may illuminate higher claims, but they may not overrule them.

### 6.3 Claim Classification

Each significant claim should be classifiable as one of the following:

1. Biblical claim: directly grounded in Scripture.
2. Doctrinal claim: grounded in received Christian teaching.
3. Historical claim: grounded in historical evidence.
4. Interpretive claim: a reading of patterns, symbols, or events.
5. Synthetic claim: an integration of multiple disciplines.
6. Speculative claim: a plausible but non-settled proposal.
7. Pastoral claim: guidance for spiritual discernment or practice.

High-risk claims must be explicitly classified in prose or notes.

### 6.4 Christological Resolution

Every chapter must end in Christological resolution. This does not mean a decorative paragraph about Jesus. It means the argument must show how Christ:

1. Exposes the false order.
2. Bears or unmasks the false sacrifice.
3. Disarms the powers.
4. Restores the created good.
5. Reforms the human person and community.

### 6.5 Speculation Policy

Speculative synthesis is permitted when useful, but must be marked and disciplined.

Speculation must:

1. Be named as speculation.
2. Remain subordinate to Scripture.
3. Avoid binding the reader's conscience.
4. Avoid sensationalism.
5. Include at least one serious objection.
6. Explain what evidence would weaken the proposal.

## 7. Research Standards

### 7.1 Source Hierarchy

Preferred sources:

1. Biblical text.
2. Primary theological sources.
3. Primary historical documents.
4. Peer-reviewed scholarship.
5. Reputable academic or ecclesial reference works.
6. Well-documented journalism for contemporary events.
7. Secondary interpretive essays.

Avoid relying on:

1. Unsourced internet claims.
2. Decontextualized quotations.
3. Viral summaries.
4. AI-generated citations.
5. Polemical claims without primary support.

### 7.2 Minimum Research Package

A major chapter should normally include:

1. At least five primary biblical passages or a reason fewer are needed.
2. At least two historical-theological witnesses when relevant.
3. At least three historical or contemporary sources when making historical claims.
4. At least one serious objection.
5. A bibliography or reference list.

### 7.3 Primary Source Rule

When a chapter makes a claim about a figure, council, law, confession, sermon, papal document, political speech, or historical event, use a primary source whenever reasonably available.

### 7.4 Contemporary Claims

Claims about current events, laws, organizations, public figures, or institutional behavior should be dated and sourced. Agents must browse or otherwise verify current information when the claim is temporally unstable.

### 7.5 Medical and Psychological Claims

The Chemical Temple framework may draw from psychology, trauma studies, addiction studies, and neuroscience, but must not present itself as medical advice.

Required language for high-stakes sections:

This theological analysis is not medical advice and should not replace care from qualified clinicians, pastors, or other appropriate professionals.

## 8. Citation Standards

### 8.1 Default Style

Use readable Markdown footnotes for essays and internal documents. Use Chicago-style bibliography entries when a chapter becomes formal enough to require a bibliography.

Example:

```markdown
The law functions as a tutor toward Christ.[^galatians-tutor]

[^galatians-tutor]: Galatians 3:24.
```

### 8.2 Scripture Citation

Scripture citations should include book, chapter, and verse. Translation should be named when quoting at length or when wording matters.

Acceptable:

1. Romans 3:19-20.
2. Romans 3:19-20, NKJV.
3. "that every mouth may be stopped" (Romans 3:19, NKJV).

### 8.3 Historical Sources

Historical citations should identify:

1. Author or issuing body.
2. Work or document.
3. Date when relevant.
4. Section, chapter, paragraph, canon, or page where possible.
5. Translator or edition when relevant.

### 8.4 AI Disclosure

When AI agents materially contribute to a chapter, the working notes should preserve:

1. Agent role.
2. Prompt or prompt version.
3. Inputs used.
4. Output date.
5. Human editor decision.

Public disclosure may be handled at the project level unless a venue requires document-level disclosure.

## 10. AI Agent Specification

Agents are assistants, not authorities. They produce research, maps, drafts, objections, diagrams, and validation reports for human review.

### 10.1 Universal Agent Rules

Every agent must:

1. Use the canonical ontology.
2. Classify claims when stakes are high.
3. Preserve uncertainty.
4. Cite sources or flag missing citations.
5. Avoid inventing sources.
6. Distinguish quotation from paraphrase.
7. End with unresolved questions and validation risks.
8. Keep Christological resolution central when drafting.

### 10.2 Agent Roles

Canonical agents:

1. Biblical Agent.
2. Historical Theology Agent.
3. Philosophy Agent.
4. Psychology Agent.
5. Anthropology Agent.
6. Systems Mapping Agent.
7. Diagram Agent.
8. Critique Agent.
9. Editorial Agent.
10. Canonical Integration Agent.

### 10.3 Agent Pipeline

Default pipeline:

1. Biblical Agent.
2. Historical Theology Agent.
3. Philosophy Agent.
4. Psychology Agent.
5. Anthropology Agent.
6. Systems Mapping Agent.
7. Critique Agent.
8. Diagram Agent.
9. Editorial Agent.
10. Canonical Integration Agent.

The Critique Agent should run before final editorial integration.

### 10.4 Agent Output Contract

All agent outputs should include:

1. Agent name.
2. Task.
3. Inputs.
4. Summary.
5. Findings.
6. Citations.
7. Claim classifications.
8. Risks.
9. Open questions.

## 11. Diagram Specification

Diagrams must clarify conceptual relationships without pretending to prove the argument.

### 11.1 Diagram Types

1. Ontology diagram.
2. Apostasy cycle.
3. Liturgy-to-formation map.
4. Sacrificial system map.
5. Historical mirror timeline.
6. Concept dependency graph.
7. Restoration map.

### 11.2 Diagram Rules

Diagrams should:

1. Use canonical terms.
2. Include directionality.
3. Distinguish creation, fall, embodiment, sacrifice, and restoration.
4. Avoid visual clutter.
5. Include a caption explaining limits.
6. Link back to a chapter section.

### 11.3 Visual Grammar

Recommended semantic styling:

1. Created good: green or neutral.
2. Fallen power: red or dark.
3. Disease Logos: amber or warning.
4. Fallen liturgy: orange.
5. Chemical Temple: blue.
6. Sacrifice: crimson.
7. Restoration: white, gold, or clear contrast.

No color should be the only carrier of meaning.

## 16. Versioning Rules

Use semantic versioning for the grammar:

1. Major version: changes to ontology, methodology, or project identity.
2. Minor version: new sections, terms, agents, schemas, or roadmaps.
3. Patch version: clarifications, typos, formatting, and non-substantive edits.

Every change to canonical terms must include:

1. Previous definition.
2. Proposed definition.
3. Reason for change.
4. Affected chapters.
5. Migration note.

## 18. Continuous Integration for Theological Consistency

The CI workflow should check what machines can reasonably check:

1. Required files exist.
2. Canonical terms appear consistently.
3. JSON schemas parse.
4. Roadmap YAML has expected structure.
5. Prompt files include purpose, role, workflow, output, and validation sections.
6. Markdown files avoid stale references to old project paths.
7. Chapters submitted for canonical development include Christological resolution markers.

Machines cannot fully judge truth, wisdom, charity, or doctrinal soundness. CI should therefore produce warnings and review prompts, not pretend to replace human theological judgment.

The first workflow is defined in `.github/workflows/theological-consistency.yml`.

## 19. Canonical Glossary

Apostasy: the deformation of holy gifts into systems of idolatry, self-justification, control, and pride.

Chemical Temple: the embodied human person formed through memory, attention, desire, neurochemistry, habit, emotion, trauma, imagination, and worship.

Christological resolution: the final movement of an argument in which Christ exposes, judges, disarms, and restores the corrupted order under analysis.

Created good: a reality, capacity, institution, symbol, or vocation that is good by origin and divine intention.

Disease Logos: the false symbolic order proclaimed by fallen powers.

Fallen liturgy: an embodied pattern that incarnates a Disease Logos and forms people into its worship.

Fall of the powers: the movement by which created governors become self-referential and demand ultimate loyalty.

Logos: God's ordering Word, finally identified with Christ.

Nehushtan: a holy instrument detached from God and treated as a controllable object of power.

Principality: a created governing power intended to order a domain of creation toward God.

Restoration: Christ's work of exposing false orders, disarming powers, healing persons, and returning created goods to their proper end.

Sacrifice: the impulse toward self-offering, true when ordered to God and distorted when given to idols.

Scapegoat: the person or group onto whom a fallen system transfers guilt so it can preserve itself without repentance.

## 21. Canonical Analysis Questions

Every chapter should be able to answer:

1. What created good is being examined?
2. Which principality or governing power orders this domain?
3. How has that power fallen?
4. What Disease Logos is proclaimed?
5. What fallen liturgies embody it?
6. How is the Chemical Temple formed?
7. What sacrificial system emerges?
8. Who or what becomes the scapegoat?
9. What historical evidence supports the pattern?
10. What objections or rival readings must be faced?
11. How does Christ expose the system?
12. How does Christ restore the created good?

## 22. Acceptance Standard for New Canonical Work

A new chapter, diagram, prompt, schema, or roadmap item is ready for canonical inclusion when:

1. It uses canonical terms consistently.
2. It identifies its claim types.
3. It cites sources proportionate to the claims.
4. It distinguishes doctrine from synthesis.
5. It includes objections or cautions.
6. It resolves in Christ.
7. It passes automated checks where applicable.
8. A human editor has reviewed the work.

## 23. Amendment Process

Proposed amendments should be submitted as focused changes.

An amendment must include:

1. Summary.
2. Rationale.
3. Affected files.
4. Affected canonical terms.
5. Migration notes.
6. Validation result.

Breaking changes to the ontology require a major version bump.
