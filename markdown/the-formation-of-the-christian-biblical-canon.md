# The Formation of the Christian Biblical Canon: A Comparative Study

Source: `raw/The Christian Canon.md`

For the first four centuries of the Christian Church, there was no single, universally bound volume known as "The Bible." Instead, local congregations shared collections of sacred scrolls and codices. The process of defining a fixed biblical canon—determining which books were divinely inspired and authoritative—unfolded over centuries, driven by theological debates, regional councils, and historical divisions.

Today, Christianity is broadly divided among three major traditions, each possessing a distinct biblical canon: **Eastern Orthodoxy, Roman Catholicism, and Protestantism**.

---

1\. The Eastern Orthodox Canon: An Open Liturgical Tradition

The Process

The Eastern Orthodox Church treats the biblical canon as an organic, living part of Holy Tradition rather than a legalistic checklist. Because Eastern Orthodoxy has no centralized, top-down legislative authority equivalent to the Roman Papacy, its canon solidified through a slow process of liturgical consensus and local synods. A book's canonicity is fundamentally tied to its historical use in the corporate worship of the Church.

Key Dates & Milestones

* **AD 367 (The New Testament Foundation)**: St. Athanasius, the Bishop of Alexandria, issued his *39th Paschal Letter*. This document provides the earliest known list matching the exact **27-book New Testament** used by all major traditions today.  
* **AD 692 (The Trullan Council)**: Also known as the Quinisext Council, this assembly in Constantinople formally endorsed all previous regional canon lists simultaneously. Rather than harmonizing the slight variations among those historical lists, the council ratified them collectively. This created a framework where minor regional variations could coexist under a shared umbrella of tradition.  
* **AD 1672 (The Synod of Jerusalem)**: In response to Protestant theologians who rejected the deuterocanonical texts, this regional council officially reaffirmed the broader Old Testament canon, explicitly defending the authority of books like Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, and Maccabees.

The Final Product

The Eastern Orthodox Old Testament is based on the **Septuagint (LXX)**, the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures used by the early Church. Because the Septuagint is larger than the Hebrew Bible, the Orthodox Old Testament contains the most books of any major tradition.

Due to its decentralized structure, minor variations exist between self-governing (autocephalous) Orthodox national churches. For example:

* The **Greek Orthodox** Bible includes **79 books** total (4 Maccabees is included as an appendix).  
* The **Slavic/Russian Orthodox** Bible includes **3 Esdras** (called 2 Esdras in Western contexts) and **4 Esdras**.  
* **Liturgical Distinction**: While the Book of Revelation is recognized as canonical Scripture, it is the only New Testament book never read aloud during Eastern Divine Liturgies.

---

2\. The Roman Catholic Canon: A Dogmatic Closure

The Process

For over a thousand years, the Western (Latin) Church operated on a stable, widely accepted list of books. However, this canon was not formally declared an infallible dogmatic article of faith until the 16th century. When Protestant Reformers challenged the authority of several Old Testament books, the Roman Catholic Church responded by formally and permanently locking its canon, attaching strict theological penalties to any deviation.

Key Dates & Milestones

* **AD 382–419 (Early Western Consensus)**: The Western canon was standardized through a series of regional synods, including the Council of Rome (382), the Council of Hippo (393), and the Councils of Carthage (397 and 419). Guided by figures like St. Augustine, these councils approved the **73-book list** used by Catholics today. Pope Innocent I confirmed this list in AD 405\.  
* **AD 1442 (The Council of Florence)**: During an effort to reunite the Western and Eastern Churches, the council issued the papal bull *Cantate Domino*, which formally listed all 73 books as inspired scripture.  
* **April 8, 1546 (The Council of Trent)**: In direct response to the Protestant Reformation, the Ecumenical Council of Trent issued the decree *De Canonicis Scripturis*. This decree definitively closed the Catholic canon, declared all 73 books equally inspired, and pronounced an *anathema* (solemn excommunication) on anyone who refused to accept them.

The Final Product

The Roman Catholic Bible contains exactly **73 books**: **46 in the Old Testament** and **27 in the New Testament**.

The Catholic Church categorizes the books disputed by Protestants as the **Deuterocanon** ("second canon"). These seven books—Tobit, Judith, 1 and 2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), and Baruch, along with additions to Daniel and Esther—are fully integrated into the Old Testament sequence rather than isolated in an appendix. The Council of Trent also declared St. Jerome’s Latin **Vulgate** translation to be the official, authoritative text for public teaching and liturgy.

---

3\. The Protestant Denominations: A Return to the Hebrew Text

The Process

The Protestant canon emerged from the theological convictions of the 16th-century Reformers, chief among them Martin Luther. Driven by the principle of *Sola Scriptura* (Scripture Alone), Reformers re-evaluated the canon based on historical origins and internal theology. They chose to reject the books of the Old Testament that were not part of the standard Hebrew Bible (the Palestinian Jewish canon), viewing them as human historical writings rather than divinely inspired Scripture.

Key Dates & Milestones

* **AD 1534 (Luther’s Bible Appendix)**: Martin Luther published his complete German Bible translation. He removed the seven deuterocanonical books from the Old Testament sequence and placed them in a separate appendix labeled **"Apocrypha"** ("hidden things"). He defined them as books that are "not held equal to the Sacred Scriptures, and yet are useful and good for reading."  
* **AD 1563/1571 (The Anglican Definition)**: The Church of England established its position in *Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles*. It listed the 39 books of the Hebrew Bible as authoritative Scripture, stating that the Apocrypha could be read for moral instruction but never to establish church doctrine.  
* **AD 1647 (The Westminster Confession)**: This foundational document for Presbyterians, Congregationalists, and Baptists formally closed the Protestant canon. It declared the Apocrypha to be "of no authority in the Church of God," stripping it of any scriptural status.  
* **AD 1826 (The Printing Shift)**: For nearly three centuries, most Protestant Bibles still printed the Apocrypha section between the Testaments. In 1826, the *British and Foreign Bible Society* permanently stopped funding the printing of Bibles containing these books. This economic and practical decision rapidly established the modern, strict 66-book format in the English-speaking world.

The Final Product

The standard Protestant Bible contains exactly **66 books**: **39 in the Old Testament** and **27 in the New Testament**.

The New Testament matches the Catholic and Orthodox traditions perfectly. However, the Old Testament mirrors the shorter Hebrew Bible, completely omitting Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and the books of Maccabees, as well as the Greek additions to Esther and Daniel.
