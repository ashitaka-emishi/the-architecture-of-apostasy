# When Rule Becomes Devouring: Entrusted Authority and the Morphology of Rebellious Rule

Source: `raw/When_Rule_Becomes_Devouring_Submission.pdf`

Abstract. Biblical and Second Temple Jewish texts depict corrupted power through different images: acquisitive kings, self-magnifying empires, false shepherds, devouring beasts,
and insatiable giants. This article proposes a Christian theological morphology, developed
through canonical and Second Temple comparison, for comparing these figures without collapsing their historical settings, literary functions, kinds of authority, or canonical status.
Its central claim is that the fullest form of predatory rule identified here is a directional reversal of entrusted authority: persons and goods placed under authority for a proper end
are reinterpreted as resources for the preservation, enlargement, exaltation, or appetite
of the ruling power itself. Ezekiel 34 supplies the controlling instance because derivative
office, proper purpose, violated limits, reversed direction, judgment, and restored shepherding converge within a single oracle. Other texts participate analogically rather than
uniformly. Deuteronomy 17 supplies a grammar of bounded kingship; 1 Samuel 8 depicts
royal appropriation; Isaiah 10 portrays providential instrumentality appropriated through
self-magnification; Daniel 7 gives predatory imperial exercise a beastly form; and 1 Enoch
functions as a Second Temple limit case intensifying disproportion and consumption without depicting entrusted political office. The comparison is governed by four coordinates:
derivative status, limits, purpose, and direction. The article argues that this morphology
identifies something more specific than domination or exploitation alone: the transformation of beneficiaries or entrusted goods into means of sustaining the authority that was
ordered toward their proper good. Within Christian theological construal of the canonical
materials, the New Testament presents the definitive contrast in the messianic kingship of
Christ, especially the Son of Man who serves, the Good Shepherd who gives his life for the
sheep, the obedient Son, and the slain yet reigning Lamb. The result is not an argument
against authority, coercion, judgment, or institutional maintenance as such, but a distinction between rightly ordered derivative rule and authority that makes its own preservation
or enlargement a governing end.
Keywords: biblical theology; authority; kingship; political theology; Ezekiel 34; Daniel 7; 1
Enoch; Christology

1. Introduction: The Problem of Direction
Biblical texts describe the corruption of power in markedly different ways. A king accumulates. Another king takes. An empire gathers nations and removes boundaries. Shepherds
feed themselves from the flock. A beast devours the earth. Giants exhaust human production and finally consume human beings. These figures do not belong to one literary genre,
historical setting, or demonstrated genealogy. Nor do they exercise the same kind of authority. Yet they repeatedly portray power becoming self-referential: what lies under, within,
or before a ruling power increasingly comes to be interpreted according to its usefulness
to that power.
This article argues that the clearest form of this corruption is a directional reversal of entrusted authority. Authority that is derivative, bounded, and ordered toward a proper end
becomes possessive when persons or goods under its charge are treated primarily as means
of sustaining the authority itself. At its extreme, possessive authority becomes devouring
authority.
The point requires precision. Possessive authority does not mean merely possessing an office, exercising jurisdiction, maintaining an institution, using resources, levying obligations,
punishing wrongdoing, or exercising coercive force. Biblical texts do not identify authority itself with corruption. They assume the legitimacy of rule, judgment, protection, shepherding, and dominion. The relevant question is one of ordering: Does the maintenance
of authority remain subordinate to the end for which authority exists, or do preservation,
enlargement, exaltation, and appetite become governing ends in themselves?
That distinction identifies something more specific than several familiar descriptions of
corrupt power. Domination identifies the subordination of others to a ruling will. Exploitation identifies the unjust extraction of benefit. Augustine’s libido dominandi describes the
self-exalting lust to dominate, while modern critiques of political sacralization describe the
capacity of political orders to gather ultimate loyalties toward themselves.1 The morphology proposed here overlaps with these categories but isolates a narrower relational change:
those for whom an authority exists, or goods entrusted to its use, become those from whom
the authority feeds. The distinctive analytical contribution therefore lies in the category of
directional reversal.
These neighboring categories answer different analytical questions. Libido dominandi
names disordered desire within the ruler; domination names a relation of subordination;
exploitation names unjust extraction; political sacralization names the displacement of
ultimate loyalty toward a political order. Directional reversal, by contrast, asks what
happens to the purpose-bearing relation itself: whether those toward whose good an
authority is ordered remain beneficiaries of that authority or become the material from

Four coordinates govern the comparison.
Derivative status asks how a power stands in relation to an order it does not create for itself.
This category includes acknowledged derivation, accountability, and operative allegiance.
It is preferable to a simple question of metaphysical “source,” since biblical texts can simultaneously affirm divine sovereignty and condemn the proximate allegiance and exercise of
a rebellious power.
Limits concern the boundaries within which authority or power may properly operate.
Purpose concerns the end for which an office, commission, or form of rule exists. Wherever
possible, that end should be established from the text’s own representation of the office or
commission rather than supplied independently by the interpreter. Where a proper end is
only canonically inferred or theologically construed, that level of inference should be stated
explicitly.
Direction concerns the relational movement of authority: whether power and resources
remain ordered toward the proper end of the office and the good of those appropriately
served by it, or whether those persons and goods are increasingly redirected toward the
preservation and enlargement of the ruling order itself.
These coordinates do not appear with equal force in every text. That inequality is not a
defect in the comparison; it is one of its controls. This article therefore distinguishes three
levels of participation in the morphology.
First, full custodial deformation occurs where an entrusted office ordered toward the good
of those under it is reversed so that the beneficiaries become resources for the officeholder.
Ezekiel 34 is the controlling instance.
Second, instrumental-power deformation occurs where a derivative power is presented as
serving a bounded providential purpose yet pursues a self-magnifying intention beyond it.
Isaiah 10 provides the clearest example. Assyria is not a shepherd failing to nurture a flock,
nor does Isaiah depict it as consciously receiving and accepting an entrusted office; it is a
providential instrument of judgment whose own intention exceeds the purpose for which
it is used.
Third, predatory intensification occurs where imagery of beastliness, disproportion, appetite, or consumption magnifies selected features of corrupted power without reproducing
the full custodial structure. Daniel’s beasts and Enoch’s giants belong here.
This hierarchy prevents comparison from becoming identity. The argument does not claim
that Samuel’s king, Isaiah’s Assyria, Ezekiel’s shepherds, Daniel’s beasts, and Enoch’s giants
are the same kind of figure. Nor does canonical juxtaposition establish literary dependence.

The originality claimed here is synthetic and conceptual rather than the discovery of previously unnoticed meanings in individual passages. Scholarship has long examined royal
accumulation, contested monarchy, failed shepherding, beastly empire, Enochic appetite,
and Christological rule within their respective contexts. At the center of the comparison is a
recurring relational change. In its fullest instances, what is received under a prior order is

The argument distinguishes four registers. Historical exegesis attends to each text in its literary and historical setting; canonical comparison places biblical texts beside one another
without making juxtaposition evidence of authorial dependence; Second Temple comparison permits Jewish texts such as 1 Enoch to function here as historically and conceptually
relevant comparanda rather than as part of the canonical argument; Christian theological construal asks how the canonical materials are subsequently read within Christian
claims about Christ and divine rule. The analytical vocabulary remains secondary to the
texts’ own language of taking, arrogance, rebellion, oppression, false shepherding, beastliness, blasphemy, devouring, judgment, service, and obedience.2
The article therefore proceeds differently from a simple canonical survey. Ezekiel 34 first
establishes the controlling morphology. Genesis and Deuteronomy then provide a compressed canonical grammar of derivative and bounded rule; Samuel shows royal appropriation; Isaiah clarifies the deformation of instrumental power; Daniel and 1 Enoch function
as intensifications and limit cases. The New Testament material then asks how the Christian
canon construes the restoration and judgment of rule in relation to Christ.

The following accusations expose the reversal: the shepherds eat the fat, clothe themselves

That language is decisive. The shepherds have not merely governed badly. The relation
between ruler and ruled has changed its direction. Those entrusted to the shepherds have
become material from which the shepherds feed.
The four coordinates are unusually visible.
Derivative status: the shepherds hold an office for which they remain answerable to YHWH.
Their authority is neither self-originating nor ultimate.
Limits: the flock is under their charge but is not therefore available for unrestricted consumption. Jurisdiction is not ownership in the possessive sense developed here.
Purpose: the repeated verbs of the chapter identify what shepherding should accomplish—
feeding, strengthening, healing, binding, seeking, rescuing, and protecting.
Direction: proper shepherding directs authority toward the life of the flock. Corrupted
shepherding reverses that movement. Life, goods, and productive capacity flow from the
flock toward the shepherds.
This last coordinate gives the article its central analytical category. The sheep cease to function primarily as those for whom the shepherds exercise office and become those from
whom the shepherds sustain themselves.
The corruption is therefore more specific than generic oppression. Oppression may describe its effects, but Ezekiel exposes the internal inversion of office. A relation of charge
becomes a relation of consumption. Authority becomes possessive when it treats what is
under its care principally in terms of its usefulness to the ruling power.
Ezekiel also supplies the positive form and thereby establishes an important control on
the argument. YHWH does not answer corrupt shepherding by abolishing shepherding.

3. Derivative and Bounded Rule: Genesis 1 and Deuteronomy 17
The canonical background can be stated briefly. Genesis 1 places human dominion within a
creation humans neither originate nor own absolutely. Dominion is given by divine speech
and blessing (Gen. 1:26-28). Genesis is not thereby presenting a political constitution, and
the present argument does not require a comprehensive account of the imago Dei. It requires only the more modest claim that human agency is creaturely and received within
an already ordered creation.5
That grammar matters because the biblical problem is not power as such. Human dominion
is given. Psalm 72 can pray for a king who judges justly, defends the poor, delivers the needy,
and crushes the oppressor (Ps. 72:1-4). Authority can protect and judge precisely because
righteous rule is not reducible to noninterference or self-sacrifice.
Deuteronomy 17 sharpens this creaturely grammar politically. Israel’s king is permitted to
hold genuine office, but his authority is bounded. He is not to multiply horses, wives, silver,
or gold for himself (Deut. 17:16-17). He must remain under Torah, learn the fear of YHWH,
and resist self-elevation.
Deuteronomy 17:20: “neither exalting himself above other members of the community” (NRSVue)
The text places restrictions precisely where royal power can turn office into enlargement.
The king remains a member of the covenant community rather than becoming the source

4. From Bounded Kingship to Royal Appropriation: 1 Samuel 8
First Samuel 8 portrays a movement from displaced confidence to appropriation. Israel
requests a king “like other nations” (1 Sam. 8:5), and YHWH interprets the request theologically:
1 Samuel 8:7: “they have rejected me from being king over them” (NRSVue)
The problem cannot simply be the existence of monarchy, since the wider biblical tradition
can envision legitimate kingship. The narrative concern is the terms on which the people
seek it. They want a visible ruler who will “go out before us and fight our battles” (8:20).
The king is sought as a concentrated center of security.
Samuel answers by describing what such concentrated kingship can take.
The king will take sons for chariots and military service, daughters for royal service, the
best fields and vineyards, portions of produce, servants, livestock, and finally a tenth of the
flocks. The catalogue ends:
1 Samuel 8:17: “you shall be his slaves” (NRSVue)
The rhetorical movement matters. Israel seeks a king who will act for the people; Samuel
warns that the people and their productive goods can instead become resources of the royal
order. Their sons become military capacity. Their daughters become royal labor. Their land
and produce move toward the court. Their servants and animals are absorbed into royal
use. Finally, they themselves become servants of the king.
This is not yet Ezekiel 34’s complete custodial morphology. Samuel’s point is framed
through the danger of concentrated royal appropriation, not through a developed
shepherd-office analogy. Yet the directional similarity is substantial: people who seek the

5. Instrumental Power and Self-Magnification: Isaiah 10 and 14
Isaiah 10 requires a different category. Assyria does not hold a custodial office over Judah
analogous to Ezekiel’s shepherds. It functions instead as an instrument of divine judgment.
Isaiah 10:5: “Ah, Assyria, the rod of my anger” (NRSVue)
The instrumentality here is providential rather than administrative: Isaiah does not portray
Assyria as consciously receiving an entrusted office and then violating its terms. The power
is instead used within divine judgment. The decisive distinction appears in verse 7: “this is
not what he intends.” Assyria acts within a purpose it does not author while pursuing an
intention of its own—destruction, accumulation, and self-magnification.
The ruler boasts:
Isaiah 10:13: “I have removed the boundaries of peoples” (NRSVue)
He attributes his accomplishments to his own strength and wisdom, gathers nations as one
gathers abandoned eggs, and treats the political world as material available for collection
(10:13-14). Isaiah then exposes the arrogance through the analogy of an ax boasting against
the one who wields it (10:15).
The deformation here is best described as instrumental-power deformation. Assyria is genuinely instrumental within divine judgment, but it acts as though the instrument were the
author of its own power and the possessor of its results. Its intention exceeds the purpose
that gives its action a place within God’s judgment and converts providential instrumentality into a self-magnifying imperial project.
This case clarifies why the first coordinate should be formulated as derivative status rather
than simple “source.” The problem is not that Assyria somehow exists outside God’s sovereign ordering. Isaiah explicitly says the opposite. The problem is that the power does
not acknowledge or operate according to its derivative status. It interprets power as selfgenerated achievement and the world as available for its enlargement.
Isaiah 14 intensifies the same problem vertically. The taunt is addressed to the king of Babylon (Isa. 14:4). The present argument does not depend on identifying that king ontologically
with Satan or another rebellious heavenly being. The poem nevertheless casts royal arrogance in cosmic and mythic language: ascent to heaven, the stars of God, the mount of
assembly, and resemblance to the Most High.10
Isaiah 14:14: “I will make myself like the Most High.” (NRSVue)
Isaiah 10 and 14 therefore disclose two forms of violated limit. Isaiah 10 depicts horizontal expansion: boundaries, treasures, peoples, and kingdoms become objects of collection.

6. Predatory Intensification: Daniel 7 and 1 Enoch
6.1 Daniel 7: Beastly Empire
Daniel 7 moves from political and administrative description into symbolic morphology.
The vision presents successive kingdoms as beasts rising from the sea (Dan. 7:2-7), and
the interpretation within the chapter identifies the beasts as kings and kingdoms (7:17, 23).
In the conventional critical reconstruction, the vision is situated within the Hellenistic crisis and the little horn is associated with Antiochus IV Epiphanes.11 Whatever subsequent
canonical uses are made of the imagery, its political reference is therefore internal to the
chapter itself.
The fourth kingdom is described in predatory terms:
Daniel 7:23: “devour the whole earth” (NRSVue)
It devours, tramples, and breaks apart (7:7, 19, 23). Daniel therefore contributes not the full
custodial sequence of Ezekiel 34 but an apocalyptic intensification of predation. Imperial
exercise receives the body of a beast.
Read canonically alongside Genesis 1, the imagery permits a theological contrast without
requiring Genesis 1 to be Daniel’s controlling literary intertext. Genesis depicts human beings receiving dominion within God’s creation. Daniel depicts human kingdoms as animals
while “one like a human being” receives dominion before the Ancient One (Dan. 7:13-14).
The kingdom is also given to “the holy ones of the Most High” (7:18, 22, 27).
The identity and representative function of the humanlike figure have been extensively
debated, with individual, corporate, angelic, and representative proposals continuing in
scholarship.12 The present comparison need not resolve those questions. At the symbolic

6.2 1 Enoch: A Limit Case of Appetite
The Book of the Watchers is an even clearer limit case because its giants do not hold entrusted governing office. It should therefore not be treated as an instance of failed kingship.
Its value for the comparison lies elsewhere: it gives disproportion and appetite a monstrous
body.
The Watchers transgress a divinely ordered boundary, take human women, transmit illicit
knowledge, and produce giant offspring (1 En. 6-8). The giants’ appetite becomes one of
their defining narrative features.13
1 Enoch 7:3: “They were devouring the labor of all the sons of men” (NickelsburgVanderKam)
The sequence then escalates. Human production is no longer sufficient to sustain the giants.
They begin consuming human beings, violence multiplies, blood is shed, and the cry of the
earth rises toward heaven (1 En. 7:3-6; 8:4; 9:1-3).
The sequence can be summarized:
boundary violation → disproportion → appetite → exhaustion of production → consumption of life → cry → judgment
This does not make the giants political allegories or coded Assyrian kings. Amar Annus
has argued that the Watcher tradition transforms Mesopotamian traditions concerning antediluvian sages and their giant offspring.14 That discussion concerns the formation of the
tradition, not a hidden political identity.

7. Christ and the Restoration of Dominion
Within a Christian canonical reading, the answer to disordered authority is not simply an
abstract theory of better governance. The New Testament locates the restoration and final
judgment of rule in the unique kingship of Christ. That claim must be made carefully. Christ
is not merely one ethical ruler among others, nor is his messianic kingship a political program that creaturely governments simply reproduce. His relation to the Father, his identity
as the incarnate Son, his redemptive death, his eschatological judgment, and his universal
reign are unique.
Nevertheless, several New Testament texts provide a canonical inversion of the directions
identified above.
7.1 The Son of Man Who Serves
In Mark 10, James and John seek elevated places in Jesus’ glory. Jesus answers by contrasting the familiar pattern of Gentile domination with the ordering of authority among his
disciples:
Mark 10:42-43: “those whom they recognize as their rulers lord it over them …
But it is not so among you” (NRSVue)
The alternative culminates in the Son of Man:
Mark 10:45: “For the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve and to give
his life a ransom for many.” (NRSVue)
Matthew Thiessen has located Mark 10:45 within Roman political and sacrificial discourse;
the present argument makes a more limited use of the passage, emphasizing Mark’s explicit
contrast between domination, service, and the Son of Man’s self-giving.15

7.2 The Good Shepherd Who Gives Himself
John 10 brings the contrast with Ezekiel 34 into sharper focus.
John 10:11: “The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” (NRSVue)
Ezekiel 34 has long been recognized as a major scriptural background for the Johannine
shepherd discourse.16 The structural reversal is direct. Ezekiel’s corrupt shepherds feed
themselves from the flock. Jesus identifies the Good Shepherd by the contrary direction: he
gives his life for the sheep (John 10:10-18).
The connection is stronger than a generalized metaphor of kindness. John presents the
shepherd’s authority in relation to the Father as well. Jesus speaks of the authority to lay
down his life and take it up again and of the command received from his Father (10:18).
The four coordinates therefore appear in restored form. The shepherd acts in ordered relation to the Father; his exercise of authority serves the purpose of giving life and protecting
the sheep; and the direction of the relation is the inverse of Ezekiel’s condemnation. The
shepherd does not consume the flock. He gives himself for it.
Within Christian theological construal, this is not merely an ethical illustration for rulers.
Christ’s death is redemptive and unique. Creaturely rulers do not atone for those under
them. Yet the messianic fulfillment of the shepherd image reveals decisively that rightful
rule is not self-consuming appetite directed at its subjects.

Isaiah’s ruler speaks in the first-person grammar of ascent: “I will ascend” and “I will make
myself like the Most High” (Isa. 14:13-14). Philippians describes an opposite movement.

8. Derivative Political Authority, Beastly Allegiance, and the Lamb
Romans 13 and Revelation 13 provide a useful final control on the category of derivative
status.
Paul can call governing authority God’s servant:
Romans 13:4: “it is God’s servant for your good” (NRSVue)
The present argument does not attempt to resolve the wider interpretive debates concerning submission, resistance, particular Roman circumstances, or the full scope of Romans
13:1-7. It uses only Paul’s explicit designation of governing authority as diakonos of God.18
That designation is important because political authority is not intrinsically beastly. It can
be described as derivative service within God’s ordering purposes.
Revelation 13, however, deliberately describes the Beast as receiving power, throne, and authority from the dragon (Rev. 13:2), while also depicting its period of authority as bounded
and permitted (13:5, 7). Revelation does not thereby establish a metaphysical dualism in
which God and the dragon are rival ultimate sources. The Apocalypse remains thoroughly
theocentric in its doctrine of sovereignty.19
This clarifies the first coordinate. Derivative status cannot be reduced to the question,
“From whom did this power metaphysically originate?” All creaturely power exists beneath
God’s sovereignty. The relevant questions include how a power understands its status,
where its proximate allegiance lies, what claims it makes, and how it exercises its authority.
Romans can therefore call governing power God’s servant, while Revelation can portray
political-imperial power as beastly in allegiance, worship, blasphemy, coercion, and violence.

9. What Devouring Authority Is—and Is Not
The comparison now permits sharper diagnostic boundaries.
First, devouring authority is not synonymous with strong authority. Biblical texts can commend effective rule, protection, punishment, and judgment.
Second, devouring authority is not synonymous with hierarchy. Ezekiel’s answer to corrupt
shepherds is restored shepherding, not the disappearance of office.
Third, devouring authority is not established merely by resource use. Every enduring institution requires resources, labor, structures, and forms of maintenance. The relevant
question is whether maintenance remains subordinate to the proper end of the institution.
Fourth, devouring authority is not established merely by taxation, military obligation, punishment, or coercion in the abstract. Those matters require their own moral and exegetical
judgments. The morphology concerns the conversion of persons and goods into means of
self-preserving or self-enlarging power.

16

Fifth, not every predatory image instantiates the full morphology. Daniel’s beasts intensify
domination and devouring. Enoch’s giants intensify appetite and disproportion. Neither
should automatically be described as a failed shepherding office.
Sixth, consumption is an extreme form, not necessarily the first stage of corruption. A
power may begin by accumulating, absorbing, centralizing, or redirecting goods toward
itself before becoming explicitly destructive. Samuel’s catalogue of taking makes that point
particularly clear.
These controls matter because without them “devouring authority” could become a rhetorical label applied to any disliked institution or exercise of power. The morphology is useful
only if it discriminates.
A responsible diagnosis therefore asks:
1. What kind of authority or power is actually present?
2. In what sense is it derivative and accountable?
3. What limits belong to its office or commission?
4. What purpose does the relevant text assign to it?
5. In what direction are persons, goods, labor, loyalty, or productive capacity moving?
6. Has institutional maintenance remained subordinate to the proper end, or has preservation and enlargement become a governing end?
7. Are those under the authority still treated as persons toward whose proper good the
office is ordered, or primarily as resources sustaining the office itself?
These questions do not mechanically resolve every political or ecclesial dispute. They provide a disciplined morphology for asking what kind of corruption a text actually portrays.

10. Conclusion: From Entrusted Rule to Devouring Rule
The comparison yields several propositions.
First, authority is derivative before it is possessive. Biblical rule exists within an order
rulers do not finally create for themselves. Genesis gives dominion; Deuteronomy bounds
kingship; Isaiah reduces imperial boasting to the absurdity of an ax boasting over the one
who wields it.
Second, the clearest sign of corruption is not power itself but misdirection. Ezekiel 34 provides the controlling form: those for whom the shepherds exist become those from whom
the shepherds feed.
Third, possessive authority is relational before it is material. The crucial change is not simply that a ruler possesses resources. It is that persons and goods under authority are reinterpreted primarily according to their usefulness to the ruling power.

17

Fourth, devouring is the extreme form of a process that may begin with appropriation or
self-magnification. Samuel’s king takes. Isaiah’s empire gathers and boasts. Daniel’s beast
devours. Enoch’s giants exhaust production and finally consume life.
Fifth, not all texts instantiate the same structure. Ezekiel provides full custodial deformation. Isaiah provides instrumental-power deformation. Daniel and Enoch provide predatory intensifications. Comparison becomes more persuasive, not less, when analogy is
graded rather than flattened.
Sixth, the biblical answer is not the abolition of authority. Ezekiel anticipates restored shepherding. Deuteronomy imagines bounded kingship. Romans can describe governing authority as God’s servant. Righteous rule may protect, judge, punish, and preserve.
Within Christian theological construal of the canonical materials, these lines culminate not
in a generalized political ethic but in the unique kingship of Christ. The Son of Man serves
rather than making greatness consist in domination. The Good Shepherd does not feed on
the sheep but gives his life for them. The incarnate Son rejects the grammar of self-ascent
and walks the path of obedience. The slain Lamb conquers, judges, and reigns.
Christ’s kingship cannot simply be reproduced by creaturely rulers. His person and redemptive work are unique. Yet his reign decisively judges the logic of devouring power. Authority does not become truly sovereign by forgetting that it is derivative. It does not become
truly great by converting those beneath it into material for itself. And it does not become
properly ordered by abandoning judgment or effective rule.
The final contrast is more exact.
It is not weak power versus strong power.
It is not noncoercion versus judgment.
It is not institutional fragility versus institutional maintenance.
It is derivative authority ordered toward its proper end under God versus authority that
makes its own preservation, enlargement, exaltation, or appetite the governing end and
therefore turns what lies under its power into material for itself.
That is when rule becomes devouring.
