# One Gospel, Two Offices

*James and Paul on Grace, Faith, and Works*

A Reformed reading of the New Testament witness

![A scroll lies between two bearded men at a doorway as a shaft of light falls across the scene.](../gallery/one-gospel-two-offices-james-and-paul.jpg)

The claim that James and Paul teach rival gospels—one of works and one of grace—has a long pedigree and a stubborn afterlife. It flatters a certain kind of reader: Paul becomes the champion of effortless pardon, James the moral scold who cannot quite trust the cross. The division is convenient. It is also false. Read in their own sentences, both apostles confess the same monergism. God alone brings the dead to life. The difference is not two soteriologies but two offices of the same salvation: Paul, as a rule, addresses the ground of a sinner’s standing before God; James addresses the evidence that such standing has in fact been given. Paul is not silent about the works grace produces. James never locates those works in the creature’s autonomous will. Peter, John, the author of Hebrews, and the Lord Jesus Himself speak with one voice on the same point.

## The Apparent Collision

The collision is usually staged with two verses. Paul writes, “For we maintain that a man is justified by faith apart from works of the Law” (Rom 3:28). James writes, “You see that a man is justified by works and not by faith alone” (Jas 2:24). Set side by side without context, the sentences look like a courtroom contradiction. Add Paul’s insistence that salvation is “not of works, so that no one may boast” (Eph 2:9) and James’s demand that faith without works is “dead by itself” (Jas 2:17), and the popular contrast writes itself: grace versus performance.

That contrast survives only if one ignores what each writer is answering. Paul is dismantling the boast that a man can stand righteous before God by Torah-keeping, circumcision, or any other human contribution. James is dismantling the boast that a man can claim saving faith while his life remains untouched. One targets legalism. The other targets antinomian profession. Both target self-reliance. Neither grants the sinner a causal role in the gift that makes him alive.

## James Begins with Monergism

James is often treated as if his letter opened at 2:14. It does not. Before he ever mentions Abraham or Rahab, he has already located every good thing outside the human will.

*Every good thing given and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shifting shadow. In the exercise of His will He brought us forth by the word of truth, so that we would be a kind of first fruits among His creatures.*

— James 1:17–18

The verb is decisive. God “brought us forth.” The new birth is an act of the Father’s will, accomplished by the implanted word, not by the sinner’s decision to cooperate. The same chapter commands the reader to “receive the implanted word, which is able to save your souls” (Jas 1:21). Reception is not manufacture. The word is already planted.

Election appears with the same bluntness: “Listen, my beloved brothers: did not God choose the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom which He promised to those who love Him?” (Jas 2:5). Faith-richness is the fruit of God’s choosing, not its condition. Later James will say that God “gives a greater grace” and “gives grace to the humble” (Jas 4:6). Even the humility that receives grace is framed as the arena of a gift, not the purchase of one. If James were a theologian of meritorious works, these sentences would be an embarrassment. They are instead the letter’s foundation. The famous paragraph on faith and works is an outworking of a birth God has already accomplished.

## What James Means by “Justified”

The nerve of the supposed contradiction is the verb *dikaioō*, “to justify.” Paul ordinarily uses it for God’s forensic declaration that the ungodly are righteous in Christ, apart from works (Rom 3:24–26; 4:5; 5:1). James uses the same verb for the public vindication of a claim. The controlling question in James 2 is not “How does a guilty man obtain a righteous standing?” but “What use is it if someone *says* he has faith but he has no works? Can *that* faith save him?” (Jas 2:14). The article and the demonstrative matter. James is not asking whether genuine faith saves. He is asking whether a faith that exists only as a verbal claim—faith without works—can save. His answer is no, because such “faith” is not faith.

The illustration of the shivering brother exposes the emptiness of words without deed (Jas 2:15–16). Demonic monotheism exposes the emptiness of orthodox confession without allegiance: “You believe that God is one. You do well; the demons also believe, and shudder” (Jas 2:19). Creedal accuracy is not regeneration. Then come Abraham and Rahab. James does not rewrite Genesis 15. He quotes it: “And Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness” (Jas 2:23; Gen 15:6). The offering of Isaac in Genesis 22, decades later, is the fulfillment and perfection of that already-counted faith: “faith was working with his works, and as a result of the works, faith was perfected” (Jas 2:22). Works do not create the righteousness counted in Genesis 15. They display that the faith of Genesis 15 was living. Rahab’s reception of the spies functions the same way. In both cases “justified by works” means shown to be righteous, vindicated as a true believer, not meritoriously constituted righteous before God.

Paul himself can use *dikaioō* in a similar evidential register. When he writes that it is not the hearers of the Law who are just before God, “but the doers of the Law will be justified” (Rom 2:13), he is not smuggling works-righteousness into Romans. He is stating that eschatological acquittal will correspond to a transformed life—the life God Himself produces in those He justifies by faith. James and Paul are not fighting over the same courtroom with opposite verdicts. They are standing in adjacent rooms of the same house.

## Paul on the Works Grace Produces

Paul is the last apostle who can be conscripted for a grace that leaves the sinner unchanged. The same paragraph that forbids boasting in works assigns those works their true origin and purpose.

*For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this not of yourselves, it is the gift of God; not of works, so that no one may boast. For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand so that we would walk in them.*

— Ephesians 2:8–10

Salvation is not of works. Salvation is unto works. The works were prepared beforehand. The believer is God’s *poiēma*, His workmanship. The causality runs downhill from election and new creation, never uphill from human performance.

Titus 3 is the same architecture in pastoral form. “He saved us, not by works which we did in righteousness, but according to His mercy, through the washing of regeneration and renewing by the Holy Spirit… so that having been justified by His grace, we would become heirs according to the hope of eternal life.” Then, immediately: “those who have believed God will be intent to lead in good works” (Titus 3:5–8). Regeneration, justification by grace, and a life intent on good works are not rival systems. They are one mercy in three tenses.

Philippians joins the same two truths in a single breath: “work out your salvation with fear and trembling; for it is God who is at work in you, both to will and to work for His good pleasure” (Phil 2:12–13). The imperative rests on an indicative. The willing and the working are God’s choice and action. Galatians refuses the false choice between idle faith and legal performance: “in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision means anything, but faith working through love” (Gal 5:6). Romans 6 will not allow a justified man to remain in sin, because he has been united to Christ’s death and resurrection. The man who has died with Christ “might walk in newness of life” (Rom 6:4). Grace is not a permit. It is a power.

Paul’s confidence about the church’s future holiness is likewise monergistic: “He who began a good work in you will perfect it until the day of Christ Jesus” (Phil 1:6). The God who justifies the ungodly is the God who finishes what He starts. James is not correcting that doctrine. He is applying it to people who want the name without the newness.

## Peter: Gift First, Diligence Second

Peter writes the same order with different vocabulary. “His divine power has granted to us everything pertaining to life and godliness, through the full knowledge of Him who called us by His own glory and excellence” (2 Pet 1:3). Everything needed has already been granted. The promises make the saints “partakers of the divine nature” (2 Pet 1:4). Only then comes the chain of diligence: in faith supply moral excellence, knowledge, self-control, perseverance, godliness, brotherly kindness, love (2 Pet 1:5–7). The effort is real. The fountain is prior. Those who lack the fruit are not struggling saints who need a second, works-based gospel. They are people who have “forgotten the purification from [their] former sins” (2 Pet 1:9). Fruitlessness is amnesia about grace, not a deficiency of grace-plus-merit.

The same letter therefore urges believers to “be all the more diligent to make your calling and choosing sure” (2 Pet 1:10). Calling and election are God’s. Assurance is confirmed in the life those gifts produce. First Peter opens with new birth “according to His great mercy” and an inheritance “reserved in heaven” (1 Pet 1:3–4), then commands holiness because the One who called them is holy (1 Pet 1:15–16). Mercy first. Likeness afterward. Peter is neither Paul-against-James nor James-against-Paul. He is the New Testament’s ordinary grammar.

## John: Birth and Its Inescapable Evidence

John is, if anything, more severe than James about the impossibility of a fruitless new birth. “Everyone who has been born of God does not sin, because His seed abides in him; and he cannot sin, because he has been born of God. By this the children of God and the children of the devil are manifested” (1 John 3:9–10). The “cannot” is not sinless perfection. It is the incompatibility of regenerate life with unrepentant practice. Righteousness and brother-love are not optional decorations on a finished justification. They are how the children are manifested.

John’s first letter also binds faith and new birth together without remainder: “Whoever believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God” (1 John 5:1). Belief is evidence of a birth already given, just as loving the Father’s children is evidence of loving the Father. The order is the order of James 1:18 and Ephesians 2:4–10. God begets. The begotten believe, obey, and love. Jesus states the same monergism with the image of the vine: “apart from Me you can do nothing” (John 15:5). Fruit is required. Fruit is impossible except by remaining in the Vine. Requirement and inability meet in union with Christ, not in a negotiated synergy.

## Hebrews and the God Who Equips

The writer to the Hebrews will not let the church treat holiness as an elective. “Pursue peace with all men, and the sanctification without which no one will see the Lord” (Heb 12:14). Yet the letter’s benediction locates even that pursuit in God’s action: “Now the God of peace, who brought up from the dead the great Shepherd of the sheep through the blood of the eternal covenant, our Lord Jesus, equip you in every good thing to do His will, working in us that which is pleasing in His sight, through Jesus Christ” (Heb 13:20–21). The God who raised the Shepherd equips the sheep. He works in them what is pleasing. James’s “show me your faith by your works” and Hebrews’ “working in us that which is pleasing” are not two religions. They are command and source.

## The Reformed Grammar

The Westminster Confession simply refused the false dilemma. Faith “is the alone instrument of justification; yet is it not alone in the person justified, but is ever accompanied with all other saving graces, and is no dead faith, but worketh by love” (WCF 11.2). The Confession cites both Romans 3:28 and James 2:17, 22, 26 in the same paragraph, because it understood that the apostles were not competitors. Good works “are the fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith,” and “their ability to do good works is not at all of themselves, but wholly from the Spirit of Christ,” who works in them “to will, and to do, of His good pleasure” (WCF 16.2–3). That is Ephesians 2:10 and Philippians 2:13 in confessional dress. It is also James 1:18 wearing a Genevan collar.

Luther’s much-abused phrase belongs here rather than in the myth of a Paul-only Reformation: faith is “a living, busy, active, mighty thing.” The Reformers did not discover a gospel that James would have rejected. They recovered the distinction James and Paul already observed—between the instrument that receives Christ and the life that necessarily follows from having received Him. To collapse that distinction into “faith versus works” is to misread both men. To keep it is to let each apostle do his appointed work: Paul shutting every mouth before the judgment seat, James unmasking every mouth that claims Christ while refusing His brothers.

## Why the Confusion Persists

The confusion persists because sinners want a salvation they can manage. Legalism wants to pay. Antinomianism wants to collect without being owned. James is intolerable to the second; Paul is intolerable to the first. A church that preaches justification without regeneration will find James an enemy. A church that preaches moral renovation without imputation will find Paul an enemy. Scripture will not grant either wish. The ungodly are justified by faith apart from works of the Law, and the faith that so justifies is never a corpse. God does not justify a fiction. He justifies sinners whom He also creates anew for good works prepared beforehand.

There is irony in the popular contrast, and it ought to be named. Paul, the supposed apostle of bare grace, fills his letters with vice lists, household codes, collections for the poor, and the demand that those who have believed be careful to devote themselves to good works. James, the supposed apostle of works, traces every perfect gift, the new birth, faith’s richness, and greater grace to the Father’s unchanging will. The New Testament is not a negotiation between them. It is a single announcement: God saves by grace, through faith, unto holiness, from beginning to end, by His own power.

## Conclusion

James and Paul do not offer two doors into the kingdom. They stand at the same door and refuse two different counterfeits. Paul refuses the counterfeit of merit. James refuses the counterfeit of mere profession. Peter refuses a calling that never becomes character. John refuses a birth that never becomes love. Hebrews refuses a sanctification that the God of peace does not Himself work in the saints. Jesus refuses branches that remain fruitless while claiming the Vine.

The gospel that emerges is neither cheap nor earned. It is monergistic in its cause and evidential in its fruit. God, in the exercise of His will, brings forth a people by the word of truth. He counts faith as righteousness because that faith receives Christ. He then displays, in works He prepared beforehand, that the faith was not dead, the birth was not imaginary, and the grace was not a theory. That is not James against Paul. That is the New Testament agreeing with itself.
