# Outside the Camp, Among the Two or Three: The Organic Ekklesia, Decentralized Authority, and Prophetic Destiny of the Scattered Remnant

Source: `raw/Outside the Camp - Among the Two or Three.pdf`

![Outside the Camp](../gallery/outside-the-camp.jpg)

The previous examination established the recurring biblical pattern: organic beginning,
institutional infiltration, systemic apostasy, divine scattering, and the preservation of a hidden
remnant. It traced this arc from the wicked shepherds of Jeremiah and Ezekiel through the
striking of the Shepherd in Zechariah, the warnings of Acts 20 and Romans 11, the
shadow-versus-substance argument of Hebrews, the True Vine of John 15, and the call to go
out to Him outside the camp, bearing His reproach. The conclusion was pastoral and personal:
in an age of compromised institutional Christianity, a life in Christ is marked by abiding in the
Vine, accepting the isolation of the remnant posture, and seeking the city that has foundations
rather than the temporary security of the camp.
Yet the question remains: what form does this remnant life take once one has stepped outside
the gate? Is the scattered believer condemned to perpetual solitude? Or does Scripture itself
supply a positive, authoritative, and fully sufficient structure for those who refuse the
compromised collective? The answer is decisive. The same Lord who scatters the flock also
defines the true assembly. He places the full weight of heaven’s authority, the distribution of
spiritual gifts, the pattern of early practice, and the prophetic climax of history not in the grand
institutional machine, but in the micro-assembly of two or three gathered in His name. What
follows is an examination of that architecture—the recovery of the organic ekklesia, the
decentralization of ministry, the historical witness of the earliest communities, the linguistic
distinction between institutional knowledge and intimate knowing, the prophetic timeline of the
Fall Feasts, and the practical exercise of binding and loosing among the remnant.

The Pristine Blueprint: Where Two or Three Are Gathered
Jesus does not leave the nature of the assembly ambiguous. In the very chapter that outlines
church discipline, He defines the ekklesia with crystalline clarity:
“Now if your brother sins, go and show him his fault, between you and him alone; if
he listens to you, you have won your brother. But if he does not listen to you, take
one or two more with you, so that by the mouth of two or three witnesses every fact
may be confirmed. And if he refuses to listen to them, tell it to the church; and if he
refuses to listen even to the church, let him be to you as the Gentile and the tax
collector. Truly I say to you, whatever you bind on earth shall have been bound in
heaven; and whatever you loose on earth shall have been loosed in heaven. Again
I say to you, that if two of you agree on earth about anything that they may ask, it
shall be done for them by My Father who is in heaven. For where two or three have
gathered together in My name, I am there in their midst.” (Matthew 18:15–20, LSB)
The institutional mind reads “church” and immediately imports later developments: buildings,
boards, membership rolls, professional clergy. Yet when Jesus spoke these words, none of
those structures existed. The Greek ekklesia was a secular political term for a local assembly of
citizens called out to deliberate and decide. Jesus redefines it for His people as the gathering of

two or three in His name. That is the minimum—and the maximum—required for the full
presence of the King and the full exercise of heaven’s authority.
The modern pressure to grow a small gathering into a “real church” with a platform, a budget,
and a paid specialist inverts the Lord’s design. Scripture reveals that the micro-assembly is not
a deficient form waiting to mature into something larger. It is the original, pristine design. The
institution is the later mutation.

Ephesians 4: Every Part Does Its Share
The same principle of decentralization governs the distribution of spiritual gifts. Paul writes:
And He Himself gave some as apostles, and some as prophets, and some as
evangelists, and some as pastors and teachers, for the equipping of the saints for
the work of service, to the building up of the body of Christ, until we all attain to the
unity of the faith, and of the full knowledge of the Son of God, to a mature man, to
the measure of the stature which belongs to the fullness of Christ, so that we are no
longer to be children, tossed here and there by waves and carried about by every
wind of doctrine, by the trickery of men, by craftiness in deceitful scheming, but
speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in all aspects into Him who is the
head, that is Christ, from whom the whole body, being joined and held together by
what every joint supplies, according to the properly measured working of each
individual part, causes the growth of the body for the building up of itself in love.
(Ephesians 4:11–16, LSB)
The five functional gifts exist for one purpose: the equipping (katartismos—the mending,
aligning, and full furnishing) of the saints. The saints themselves perform the work of service.
Growth occurs not by the monologue of a professional class, but by “what every joint supplies”
and “the properly measured working of each individual part.” When an institution replaces this
lateral, participatory flow with a centralized stage production, it stunts the body and fosters
chronic immaturity. The remnant assembly recovers the original intent: every believer is
expected to bring a psalm, a teaching, a revelation, a tongue, or an interpretation (1 Corinthians
14:26). The goal of true leadership is its own functional obsolescence on a
platform—transferring operational ministry to the ordinary members until the body builds itself up
in love.

The Historical Witness: Simplicity Without Chaos
The fear that an open, non-liturgical gathering of ordinary believers will dissolve into chaos is
contradicted by the earliest post-apostolic records. The Didache (late first or early second
century) instructs communities on the reception of itinerant teachers with striking economic
clarity: an apostle may stay one day, or two if necessary; if he remains three days he is a false
prophet; if he asks for money he is a false prophet. The local assembly retains the right and
responsibility to test character and motive. Authority is not conferred by title or credential but
recognized by fruit and freedom from greed.

Justin Martyr, writing around 155 A.D., describes the Sunday gathering with equal simplicity: the
memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read “as long as time permits”; the
one who presides instructs and exhorts; all rise together and pray; bread and wine and water
are brought; those who are able and willing contribute according to their ability, and the
collection is used for orphans, widows, the sick, prisoners, and strangers. There is no
institutional overhead, no professional class extracting a living from the flock, no timed
entertainment sermon. The focus remains the extensive reading of the raw text and the mutual
care of the body. The remnant does not invent a new form; it recovers the ancient one.

Revelation 11: The Two Witnesses and the Measuring of the Temple
The prophetic climax of this architecture appears in Revelation 11. John is given a measuring
rod and commanded:
“Get up and measure the sanctuary of God and the altar, and those who worship in
it. And leave out the court which is outside the sanctuary and do not measure it, for
it has been given to the Gentiles, and they will trample the holy city under foot for
forty-two months.” (Revelation 11:1–2, LSB)
Measuring signifies divine ownership and protection. The outer court—the visible, public,
institutional manifestation—is deliberately left unmeasured and given over. Out of this
environment of institutional eviction, God raises not a massive movement but the legal
minimum:
“And I will give authority to my two witnesses, and they will prophesy for 1,260
days, clothed in sackcloth. These are the two olive trees and the two lampstands
that stand before the Lord of the earth.” (Revelation 11:3–4, LSB)
Two. The exact number required by Deuteronomy 19:15 and Matthew 18:16 for every fact to be
confirmed. Clothed in sackcloth—the uniform of mourning and detachment from the wealth of
the camp. Their power is unmediated; they stand as olive trees pouring oil directly into the
lampstands. When the beast kills them, the earth-dwellers celebrate because these two
prophets “tormented those who dwell on the earth.” After three and a half days the breath of life
enters them, they stand, and a voice from heaven says, “Come up here.” They ascend while
their enemies watch.
This is the ultimate prophetic baseline for the remnant experience. The institutional system will
claim victory over the scattered voices. It will use numbers, resources, and cultural consensus to
silence and celebrate the demise of those who refuse compromise. Yet the final verdict belongs
to the court of heaven, which vindicates the two or three who stood exclusively for the truth.

Oida and Ginosko: Tracking Truth Through Deception

Institutions survive by controlling information and creating a class of experts who possess
oida—intellectual knowledge, data, credentials, the recognition of facts. The New Testament
distinguishes this sharply from ginōskō—experiential, relational, intimate knowledge.
In John 10 the sheep follow the Shepherd because they know (oida) His voice; they will never
follow a stranger because they do not know (oida) the voice of strangers. Yet the deeper reality
is relational: “I am the good shepherd, and I know [ginōskō] My own and My own know [ginōskō]
Me” (John 10:14, LSB). A wolf may master the vocabulary, the credentials, and the institutional
data points (oida). The remnant detects the absence of relational substance and flees.
The most sobering statement of this distinction appears at the judgment:
“Many will say to Me in that day, ‘Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in Your name, and
in Your name cast out demons, and in Your name perform many miracles?’ And
then I will declare to them, ‘I never knew [ginōskō] you; depart from Me, you who
practice lawlessness.’” (Matthew 7:22–23, LSB)
These were successful institutional figures. They possessed oida about Jesus and performed
visible works in His name. Yet there was no intimate knowing. Truth is not tracked primarily
through institutional consensus or academic mastery. It is tracked through raw, vertical intimacy
with the living Voice of the Shepherd. The remnant’s defense against deception is not a superior
library; it is a superior relationship.

The Fall Feasts and the Regrafting of the Natural Branches
The same God who scatters also gathers according to His appointed times. The Spring Feasts
were fulfilled with precise historical correspondence in the first advent. The long summer
interlude corresponds to the times of the Gentiles. The Fall Feasts map the impending reversal.
Yom Teruah, the Feast of Trumpets, sounds the alarm that gathers the scattered camps and
signals the end of institutional complacency. Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, points to the
national mourning of Israel when they look upon the One they pierced (Zechariah 12:10).
Sukkot, the Feast of Tabernacles, is the ultimate non-institutional feast. God commands His
people to leave permanent dwellings and live in temporary booths, exposed to the open sky,
celebrating the final harvest and the God who tabernacled with them in the wilderness. The
institutional mind craves permanent monumental structures. God’s final appointed time
demands mobile, fragile, vertical dependency. When the King returns, the kingdoms of this
world become the kingdom of our Lord, and the tent of God is with men (Revelation 21:3). The
scattered remnant who wandered in dens and caves finds that the entire age has been moving
toward a global Sukkot.

Practical Authority in the Micro-Assembly
The fear that a small gathering lacks “covering” or legal authority is a psychological weapon of
the institution. Matthew 18 places the full power of binding and loosing—the rabbinic language

for declaring practices forbidden or permitted under Scripture—directly into the hands of the two
or three. The process of discipline begins in absolute privacy, expands to one or two witnesses,
and, if necessary, reaches the ekklesia—which Jesus has already defined as the gathering of
two or three. No bishop’s signature or denominational vote is required. Heaven recognizes the
decision made in harmony with the Word.
Agreement (symphōneō—the root of “symphony”) is not coerced consensus enforced by policy
handbooks. It is the resonant alignment of hearts submitted to the same Lord and the same text.
When two achieve this, the Father acts. The remnant assembly is not a “sub-church.” Under the
legal framework of Matthew 18 it constitutes a fully authoritative, heaven-backed court.

The Synthesized Reality
The architecture is now complete. The believer who has gone outside the camp is not left in
formless isolation. He is invited into the original design: a micro-assembly where every joint
supplies, where gifts equip the saints for the work of ministry, where the raw text is read at
length, where economic purity is guarded, where truth is tracked by intimate knowing rather than
institutional data, where the Fall Feasts announce the coming regrafting, and where two or three
hold the keys of the kingdom.
The institutional machine will continue to declare such gatherings incomplete. It will measure
validity by size, budget, visibility, and cultural alignment. Scripture measures by a different
standard: the presence of the King in the midst of those gathered in His name, the mutual
building up of the body, the refusal of compromise, and the vertical intimacy that no credential
can counterfeit.
The camp is a decaying illusion. The scattered flock, tethered to the eternal High Priest outside
the gate and gathered in the simple authority of the two or three, is the only structure that will
stand when heaven and earth are shaken. Hold fast to the substance. Maintain the harmony of
the remnant assembly. Watch the horizons for the trumpet of the Ingathering. The tent of God is
coming.
