# The Troth Does Not Grasp
### A Troth-Contextualized Sermon on Philippians 2:1–11

Before throne and pasture.  
Before fang and fleece.  
**The Troth remains.**

Paul writes to a community and commands: “Do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility regard others as better than yourselves.” He then points not to an abstract virtue but to Christ.

The movement of Philippians 2 is decisive for the Troth: Christ possesses the highest status and does not grasp it as possession. Authority is not displayed by consuming others. Christ descends, serves, takes the servant's form, remains obedient, and receives exaltation rather than seizing it.

## I. Before the Throne

Philippians 2:6 presents Christ as one who does not treat equality with God as something to exploit or grasp. Human authority is often anxious: ruler, church, nation, leader, and individual feel compelled to prove status. Christ does not need to clutch the throne.

The question therefore becomes not merely “Do I hold authority?” but **“What does my authority require others to bear so that I may continue holding it?”**

The throne may exist. Authority may be necessary. But authority that consumes others to secure itself has departed from the pattern of Christ.

## II. Before the Pasture

Paul tells the community to look to the interests of others. The pasture is supposed to shelter life, but every community is tempted to protect itself instead of the persons for whom it exists. Reputation, institution, tradition, congregation, nation, or family can survive while particular people are quietly sacrificed.

**The pasture exists for life. Life does not exist for the pasture.**

## III. He Emptied Himself

Christ does not merely refrain from domination. He moves toward service. The opposite of domination is not helplessness but strength ordered toward service.

The question for authority becomes: **Can power be exercised without becoming possession? Can strength protect without learning to love domination? Can the one who commands still wash feet?**

## IV. Before the Fang

“Let the same mind be in you that was in Christ Jesus.” The fang says that because I can wound, I can secure myself. Christ refuses to let threat determine what he becomes.

This does not require passivity before harm. It requires refusing to make the power to wound a moral identity.

**Before fang, the Troth remains.**

## V. Before the Fleece

Christ's humiliation does not make humiliation holy in itself. He is not righteous because others harmed him; he is righteous in his faithfulness through suffering.

The wound matters. Injustice matters. The victim must be heard. But suffering does not make the sufferer the source of the Good.

**Before fleece, the Troth remains.**

## VI. Obedient Unto Death

Human institutions often love the language of obedience. The obedience of Christ cannot therefore be reduced to “do what authority commands.” Christian obedience is fidelity to God even where earthly authority demands a different allegiance.

The Troth judges obedience. Obedience does not define the Troth.

## VII. The Descent

The Christic movement is downward: not grasping, emptying, serving, humbling, obeying. Human systems generally ascend, accumulate, display, and protect. Philippians reverses the architecture of greatness.

The “therefore” of exaltation matters. Christ does not seize the name above every name. It is given.

## VIII. The Name

Human powers name themselves great, holy, indispensable, chosen, victorious, necessary. Christ does not seize the name.

This produces an Unclaimed Virtue discipline:

Do not name yourself righteous. Do not name yourself the guardian of civilization. Do not name yourself the sheep. Do not name yourself the hero. Do not claim the Troth as possession.

Walk. Serve. Hear. Repent. Remain. Let judgment belong to God.

## IX. Every Knee

There is finally lordship in Philippians 2, but the Lord is the servant who refused exploitation. Christian lordship therefore judges every human theory of lordship.

The throne answers to the servant. The shepherd answers to the one who lays himself down. The commander answers to the one who washes feet. The church, nation, fellowship, and individual all answer to Christ.

## X. The Troth Is Not Ours

If we say, “We are the people of the Troth, therefore we are righteous,” we have already betrayed it. If we say our enemies are wolves and therefore our fang is holy, we have become what we oppose. If we say our suffering makes our cause pure, we have wrapped ourselves in fleece and made it a throne.

Paul gives another mind: the mind of Christ.

## XI. When We Do Not Remain

Eventually the confession comes:

**I did not remain.**

I grasped. I defended position. I preserved the pasture. I used the fang. I hid in the fleece. I mistook my judgment for God's.

The answer is not that failure did not matter. Nor is failure permitted to become the final measure.

**You are not the measure.**  
**The Troth remains.**

Because Christ remains.

## XII. Let This Mind Be in You

When you possess authority: do not grasp.  
When you belong to the pasture: do not worship the pasture.  
When you possess the fang: do not become the fang.  
When you bear wounds: do not make the fleece your throne.  
When service costs you: remain.  
When you fail: return.  
When another fails: remember that neither of you is the measure.

Let hearing become flesh. Let mercy become flesh. Let repentance become flesh. Let endurance become flesh. Let Christ be the measure.

Before throne and pasture.  
Before fang and fleece.  
**The Troth remains.**

Amen.
