# The Troth Remains  
### A Sermon on John 10:1–18

**Text: John 10:1–18**

Before throne and pasture.

Before fang and fleece.

**The Troth remains.**

Jesus speaks in John 10 of sheep, shepherds, thieves, strangers, hired hands, wolves, gates, voices, and pasture.

It is tempting to hear these words as a simple story in which everyone already knows who is who.

Christ is the shepherd.  
The faithful are the sheep.  
The enemies are the wolves.

And once we have assigned the parts, we may congratulate ourselves for standing on the correct side of the fence.

But that is precisely where this text becomes dangerous.

For Jesus does not begin by telling the sheep that they are good.

He begins by teaching them to **listen**.

> “The sheep hear his voice, and he calls his own sheep by name and leads them out.”  
> —John 10:3

The decisive question is not:

*Do I belong to the right pasture?*

The decisive question is:

**Whose voice have I learned to follow?**

For there are many voices.

The voice of the throne.

The voice of the pasture.

The voice of fear.

The voice of injury.

The voice of vengeance.

The voice that tells us we are innocent.

The voice that tells us the stranger is dangerous.

The voice that tells us obedience is righteousness.

The voice that tells us belonging is truth.

And somewhere among all these voices is the voice of the Shepherd.

The sheep know that voice.

Or they must learn it.

---

## I. Before the Pasture

Jesus says:

> “He calls his own sheep by name and leads them out.”  
> —John 10:3

Notice the direction.

The shepherd does not merely gather the sheep **into** the enclosure.

He leads them **out**.

The sheepfold is useful. It protects. It gathers. It marks a place of belonging.

But the sheepfold is not the destination.

The pasture is not the Troth.

This matters because human beings are always tempted to confuse the enclosure with the Shepherd.

We build churches, nations, orders, families, schools, armies, traditions, and communities. These things may shelter life. They may preserve memory. They may teach discipline. They may keep wolves away.

But eventually every pasture whispers the same temptation:

**Stay because you belong here.**

Christ says something harder:

**Follow because you know my voice.**

Sometimes the Shepherd leads us through the gate.

Sometimes the Shepherd leads us out of the enclosure we had mistaken for the whole world.

Therefore:

**Before pasture, the Troth remains.**

The pasture may be good.

But the pasture is not God.

The community may be beloved.

But the community is not the measure.

The institution may teach the truth.

But the institution is not Truth.

There is only one Shepherd whose voice the sheep must ultimately know.

---

## II. Before the Throne

Jesus contrasts himself with thieves and robbers.

> “All who came before me are thieves and robbers, but the sheep did not listen to them.”  
> —John 10:8

This is a claim about authority.

Many will claim the right to command the flock.

Kings claim it.

Priests claim it.

Generals claim it.

Political movements claim it.

Institutions claim it.

Even frightened crowds claim it.

But authority does not become righteous merely because authority has spoken.

The throne is not the measure.

Christ gives the measure:

> “The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.”  
> —John 10:11

That reverses the ordinary logic of power.

The false shepherd asks:

**What must the sheep sacrifice for me?**

The Good Shepherd asks:

**What must I give for the sheep?**

The false throne consumes bodies to preserve itself.

The Shepherd gives himself so that the sheep may live.

The false shepherd says:

*Your death proves your loyalty.*

Christ says:

*My self-giving reveals my love.*

This is why every throne must stand beneath judgment.

Not only cruel thrones.

Even useful thrones.

Even legitimate thrones.

Even our throne.

If authority requires us to stop hearing the cry of the person before us, then authority has begun demanding what does not belong to it.

Before throne and pasture—

**The Troth remains.**

---

## III. Before Fang

Then the wolf appears.

Jesus says:

> “The wolf snatches them and scatters them.”  
> —John 10:12

The wolf is real.

Christian mercy does not require us to pretend otherwise.

There are predators.

There are people who wound others deliberately.

There are systems that consume the weak.

There are lies designed to enslave.

There are powers that scatter communities and devour what they were entrusted to protect.

The Doctrine of Unclaimed Virtue cannot mean refusing to name evil.

The Shepherd sees the wolf.

But something strange happens when human beings encounter wolves.

We begin believing that because the wolf is evil, whatever opposes the wolf must therefore be good.

And so the fang reproduces itself.

The wolf bites.

Then we bite the wolf.

Then we learn to bite anyone who resembles the wolf.

Then we become frightened of laying down the fang because the fang has become our protection.

Eventually we cannot distinguish between protecting the sheep and loving the fang.

But Christ does not defeat the wolf by becoming a greater wolf.

The Shepherd lays down his life.

That does not mean passivity.

Jesus is not describing indifference toward destruction.

The Good Shepherd confronts the wolf precisely because the sheep matter.

But the Shepherd does not derive his identity from having an enemy.

The wolf does not tell Christ who Christ is.

Therefore the wolf must not tell us who we are.

**Before fang, the Troth remains.**

Our enemy does not determine our ethics.

Our fear does not determine our humanity.

Their wickedness does not grant us permission to become what we oppose.

---

## IV. Before Fleece

This may be the more difficult word.

Before fleece.

We understand why the fang cannot be the measure.

But why not the fleece?

Because victimhood can also become an identity through which we claim moral possession.

The sheep can begin saying:

*We were wounded; therefore we are righteous.*

*They attacked us; therefore whatever we do next is justified.*

*We suffered; therefore our judgment cannot be questioned.*

But suffering does not transform a human being into the source of the Good.

The sheep still need the Shepherd.

The wounded still require truth.

The oppressed remain capable of becoming oppressors.

The innocent remain creatures and not gods.

The fleece does not become the measure merely because the fang has pierced it.

This does not diminish suffering.

Quite the opposite.

It refuses to exploit suffering by turning the victim into an instrument of someone else's ideology.

Christ says:

> “I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly.”  
> —John 10:10

The Shepherd does not save the sheep so that they may spend eternity defining themselves by the wolf.

He saves them for life.

The wound matters.

The dead matter.

The violated matter.

The cry matters.

But the purpose of salvation is not that the wounded become permanent monuments to their wounds.

It is that they live.

Therefore:

**Before fleece, the Troth remains.**

---

## V. “I Know My Own”

Jesus says:

> “I know my own and my own know me.”  
> —John 10:14

There is something deeply personal here.

Christ does not merely know the flock.

He knows the sheep.

The collective does not swallow the person.

The pasture does not erase the name.

The Shepherd calls them individually.

This is crucial because almost every false shepherd eventually asks the individual to disappear into the whole.

For the nation.

For the race.

For the party.

For the institution.

For history.

For victory.

For the cause.

But Christ says:

**I know my own.**

The sheep is not valuable because it contributes to the pasture.

The sheep is not valuable because it strengthens the throne.

The sheep is not valuable because its death inspires the others.

The sheep is known.

The person is known.

The cry is heard.

This is why the question at the heart of the Troth cannot merely be:

**Do I bear the sign?**

The harder question is:

**What cry have I trained myself not to hear?**

Because the Shepherd hears the sheep by name.

And if we claim to follow the Shepherd while teaching ourselves that certain cries are merely noise, then we may have learned another voice.

---

## VI. The Hired Hand

Jesus then describes another figure.

Not the wolf.

Not the shepherd.

The hired hand.

> “The hired hand sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and runs away.”  
> —John 10:12

The hired hand is important because he may look like a shepherd until the cost arrives.

When service is easy, he serves.

When righteousness is admired, he appears righteous.

When loyalty brings honor, he is loyal.

Then the wolf comes.

And suddenly we discover what everything was worth.

This is where the Troth is tested.

Not when everyone agrees.

Not when virtue is rewarded.

Not when courage brings glory.

But when remaining costs something.

**The Troth is the Troth remaining.**

The hired hand does not remain.

Christ remains.

And this gives repentance its meaning.

Because each of us must eventually confess:

**I did not remain.**

I fled.

I failed.

I silenced the cry.

I protected myself.

I mistook the throne for righteousness.

I mistook the pasture for truth.

I loved my fang.

I wrapped myself in fleece.

I did not remain.

And the answer of the Gospel is not:

*Your failure did not matter.*

Nor is it:

*You are forever identical with your failure.*

The answer is:

**You are not the measure.**

**The Troth remains.**

The Shepherd remains.

And because the Shepherd remains, return remains possible.

---

## VII. “I Lay It Down of My Own Accord”

At the heart of this passage Jesus says:

> “No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord.”  
> —John 10:18

Here the Gospel reaches the deepest reversal.

Human systems repeatedly make sacred things by sacrificing people to them.

Christ reveals holiness by giving himself for people.

The throne says:

*Die for me.*

The Shepherd says:

*I lay down my life for you.*

The pasture says:

*You belong to us.*

The Shepherd calls each by name.

The fang says:

*Destroy before you are destroyed.*

The Shepherd remains faithful even before the wolf.

The fleece says:

*My suffering makes me righteous.*

The Shepherd says:

*Follow me.*

And so the cross becomes the judgment of every false shepherd.

Every institution.

Every ideology.

Every ruler.

Every congregation.

Every movement.

Every one of us.

The Good Shepherd does not ask others to bear what he himself refuses to bear.

He goes first.

---

## VIII. One Flock, One Shepherd

Jesus finally says:

> “I have other sheep that do not belong to this fold. I must bring them also.”  
> —John 10:16

That sentence should disturb every pasture convinced that God ends at its fence.

There are other sheep.

The Shepherd knows them before we do.

The Shepherd may be calling people we have not recognized.

The boundaries of our enclosure are not identical with the boundaries of his mercy.

“There will be one flock, one shepherd.”

Notice again:

Not one throne.

Not one pasture.

Not one tribe.

Not one human institution capable of claiming ownership of all the others.

**One Shepherd.**

Unity lies in the voice followed, not in the fence defended.

---

## IX. The Troth Remains

So we return to the greeting.

**Before throne and pasture.**

Because power and belonging must both kneel before the Shepherd.

**Before fang and fleece.**

Because neither our violence nor our suffering can make us the measure of righteousness.

And together:

**The Troth remains!**

When the throne falls—

The Troth remains.

When the pasture burns—

The Troth remains.

When the wolf appears—

The Troth remains.

When the sheep discovers a fang in its own mouth—

The Troth remains.

When the hired hand runs—

The Troth remains.

When we confess:

*I did not remain*—

The Troth remains.

Not because we are faithful enough.

Because the Shepherd is.

Jesus says:

> “I am the good shepherd. I know my own and my own know me.”

Therefore do not ask first whether you bear the sign.

Ask whose voice you follow.

Ask whose cry you no longer hear.

Ask what you would sacrifice to preserve your pasture.

Ask whom your throne teaches you to ignore.

Ask whether your wounds have become permission to wound.

Ask whether your righteousness survives only because no wolf has yet appeared.

And when the answer condemns you, do not flee from it.

Hear the Shepherd.

Return.

For before throne and pasture—

**Before fang and fleece—**

**The Troth remains.**

Amen.
