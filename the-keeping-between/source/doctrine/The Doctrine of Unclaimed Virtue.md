# The Doctrine of Unclaimed Virtue

Human beings must act without presuming themselves innocent, righteous, or morally complete.

Moral purity is real. It is revealed and embodied in Jesus Christ. The problem is not that virtue or moral purity do not exist, but that fallen human beings cannot securely identify their own motives, judgments, institutions, or historical causes with the purity of Christ.

Because human knowledge is partial, motives are mixed, and actions produce unintended consequences, no person can safely claim virtue as a possession. The claim to virtue must therefore be repressed—not virtue itself, but the belief that virtue belongs to us in a form that establishes innocence, superiority, or authority.

Christ is the measure of virtue. The human being is not.

## 1. Human action cannot wait for human innocence

Christ occupies the morally pure position. Human beings do not.

No fallen human actor possesses the complete purity, knowledge, freedom, and unity of will found in Christ. Human action remains entangled in ignorance, self-interest, fear, institutional pressure, bodily limitation, and unintended consequence.

Refusal to act until one has achieved moral purity is therefore a refusal to accept the human condition. It is also an action with consequences.

A person must act despite uncertainty, while measuring the action against Christ and accepting that even necessary action may contain error, selfishness, violence, or sin.

This is the proper meaning of acting boldly: not indifference toward evil, but freedom from the illusion that human purity must precede responsibility.

## 2. Virtue is revealed in Christ but cannot be claimed as possession

Courage, justice, mercy, fidelity, holiness, and love are real. They are not merely social conventions or unknowable abstractions. Their fullest form is disclosed in Christ.

The human problem is epistemic and spiritual: the individual cannot reliably pronounce themselves courageous, just, merciful, faithful, holy, or loving.

Virtue is corrupted when it becomes self-identity. Once a person believes themselves virtuous, they begin interpreting their desires as moral insight, their power as authority, and their opposition as evidence of righteousness.

The disciplined person therefore seeks conformity to Christ while refusing the title of “virtuous person.”

Virtue may be received, practiced, and manifested. It may not be possessed as personal property.

## 3. Christ judges both virtue and vice

The individual does not repress virtue because virtue is unreal. The individual represses the impulse to identify their own moral self-image with Christ.

Christ stands outside the self as judge, model, revelation, and redeemer. He prevents moral humility from collapsing into relativism because the good has been disclosed. He also prevents moral conviction from becoming self-deification because no human actor fully embodies that good.

The standard is therefore neither private conscience nor ideological necessity.

The standard is Christ.

## 4. Confession does not authorize transgression

Awareness of one’s corruption does not grant permission to do evil.

The willingness to accept guilt, condemnation, suffering, or damnation does not create moral authority. A person who says, “I know this is wrong, but I alone am willing to do it,” may be practicing a deeper form of pride: the pride of the necessary sinner.

Acknowledged corruption can become as self-exalting as proclaimed innocence.

Christ bears sin redemptively. Human beings cannot imitate that work by appointing themselves damned instruments whose guilt will save others.

No person becomes Christ by becoming monstrous on behalf of a cause.

## 5. Neither power nor sacrifice creates sovereignty

The powerful person may claim authority because they are stronger. The sacrificial person may claim authority because they have suffered more or are willing to sacrifice more.

Both claims are rejected.

Power does not establish goodness. Suffering does not establish wisdom. Sacrifice does not entitle one person to determine the sacrifice of another.

Christ’s sacrifice cannot be used to justify sacrificial political or ideological systems. His self-giving exposes and judges the human tendency to preserve sacred institutions by consuming human lives.

No individual becomes sovereign by possessing strength, enduring pain, accepting guilt, or declaring a mission necessary.

## 6. Moral uncertainty must apply to the enemy

A person cannot claim uncertainty about their own virtue while maintaining absolute certainty about the total evil of everyone they oppose.

The statement “I do not know myself to be virtuous” must not become “but I know whom Christ requires me to destroy.”

Christ provides a real standard for identifying cruelty, domination, falsehood, and injustice. Yet judgment of acts must not become the declaration that a person or category of persons is beyond redemption.

The enemy must remain human, morally accountable, and potentially redeemable.

Any ideology that makes personal goodness uncertain but enemy destruction absolute will become an ideology of purification.

## 7. The mission must remain subordinate to Christ and the person

No historical mission, political cause, institution, nation, revolution, church, or campaign may become an absolute good.

Christ alone is Lord. Every mission remains subject to his judgment.

When people are reduced to instruments, obstacles, liabilities, or acceptable losses, the mission has become an idol. A legitimate purpose must remain interruptible by truth, mercy, accountability, repentance, and the concrete needs of persons.

The mission exists to serve persons under Christ. Persons do not exist to be sacrificed to the mission.

## 8. Accountability is stronger than sincerity

Sincerity, conviction, sacrifice, and willingness to suffer do not prove that an action conforms to Christ.

The person who acts must remain corrigible. They must submit their judgment to others, hear those affected by their actions, accept limits, confess error, make restitution, and abandon a course of action when its means have corrupted its purpose.

No one may invoke urgency, exceptional circumstances, superior knowledge, personal sacrifice, or supposed obedience to Christ to place themselves beyond judgment.

A claimed revelation that eliminates accountability should be treated as evidence of idolatry rather than authority.

## 9. Grace abolishes both moral pride and heroic despair

A person is not justified by virtue, innocence, suffering, sacrifice, ideological purity, or historical success.

Justification belongs to Christ.

Therefore, human beings need neither pretend to possess his righteousness nor cultivate wickedness as proof of seriousness. They may confess wrongdoing without making wrongdoing their identity. They may act without claiming purity and repent without collapsing into despair.

Grace is not permission to sin. It is freedom from the need to make either virtue or sin the foundation of the self.

Christ, rather than the moral performance of the individual, is that foundation.

## Central Formula

Christ is morally pure.

I am not Christ.

Therefore:

Act without claiming innocence.

Seek conformity to Christ without claiming to possess his virtue.

Confess sin without converting confession into permission.

Accept responsibility without claiming sovereignty.

Judge actions without declaring persons irredeemable.

Resist evil without becoming organized by hatred of the enemy.

Serve the mission without sacrificing persons to it.

Trust Christ more than virtue, guilt, power, suffering, or success.

## Final Principle

The central danger is not merely that human beings will mistake themselves for heroes.

It is that they will identify their judgment with Christ, or make their willingness to become villains the proof that they alone are qualified to save the world.

The Doctrine of Unclaimed Virtue therefore rejects both the righteousness of the supposedly innocent ruler and the righteousness of the self-condemned transgressor.

It affirms that moral purity is real, objective, and embodied in Christ. It denies that any fallen person, institution, ideology, or historical mission may claim that purity as its possession.

Moral life consists in conformity to Christ without identification with Christ: responsible action, permanent humility, external accountability, continual repentance, protection of persons from sacrificial systems, and fidelity to a good that has been revealed but cannot be privately owned.
