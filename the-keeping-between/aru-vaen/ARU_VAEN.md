# Aru va'en

## A Keeping of the Troth

Before throne and pasture, before fang and fleece, there was the Troth.  
Before the Troth had a name, it had an ache.

## The First Keeping: Night

Cries in the dark.

At first I thought they were wolves.

Then I thought they were children.

Then I understood that some cries are older than names. They come up from the torn places of the world, from fields where blood has entered the soil, from mouths that no longer know whether they are praying or dying.

They came before dawn because before dawn is when the forest listens.

The dens grew restless. Mothers drew their young closer. Wolves who had lived through many winters lifted their heads but did not rise. Even the blind ones opened their eyes toward the border, toward that thin place where the trees ended and the mist began.

I was young then, though not as young as I wanted to be.

I am Vaelun.

A scout of the outer ring. A watcher of the boundary stones. One who keeps to the places where our scent thins and other things begin.

Yet the Vaelun belonged to more than the outer ring.

We stood at thresholds: of flesh, of grief, of the world's torn places.

Vaelun.

Brother, watcher, coal-under-ash.

So the Gravane remembered us.

Among the Vaelun I bore the office of Vaerun.

A Vaelun watched the veil. A Vaerun crossed when and where it must be crossed.

Others had borne that burden before me, and others might bear it after. There was little majesty in the office. Mostly it was mud, hunger, cold, and listening.

Still, the office mattered.

Its charge came before the hall's command.

The trust had already bound me.

Then the cries came again.

They moved through the forest like waves of sickness. The air tasted of acid and copper. Somewhere beyond the veil, something living dragged itself across leaves. Something wounded called out and was answered by something worse.

I did not want to go.

This is the first truth.

Not that I went bravely.

Not that I was eager.

I knew that answering the cry would require more than crossing a line of stones. It would ask something costly, and I could not know beforehand what faithfulness would demand.

The hall had entrusted me with the crossing. I feared the crossing would require the whole of that trust and find me wanting.

I trembled when I entered the hall. I bowed beneath the torn banners and waited before the stone seat where the Hrovan watched me, his muzzle silvered, his eyes dark with remembered winters.

The Hrovan's place was the hall, as the Vaelun's was the veil and the Vaerun's the crossing.

He bore the center when the people might otherwise forget that they were one body.

We called him lord because that was easier.

The Gravane called him Hrovan and lowered their heads, not because the seat made him righteous, but because the burden was real.

He wore no crown.

The stone, the banners, and the silence were weight enough.

"Vaerun, you have heard the cry," he said.

"Yes, my lord."

"What have you discerned?"

I had heard an ache in the cry, but I could not yet say what it required.

I did not answer.

The Hrovan rose and looked toward the names beneath the banners. They belonged to wolves who had gone beyond the veil and not returned, and to others who had returned unable to speak of what they had seen.

I had touched those names as a cub. I had thought them beautiful.

Now they looked like teeth.

Some had watched the veil. Some had crossed it. Some had borne the hall. These burdens did not ascend one into another, and none promised that its bearer had answered well.

The Hrovan turned back to me.

"Go," he said. "Seek out that which cries."

The stone seat entrusted him to answer.

It did not tell either of us whether his answer would keep faith.

So I went.

## The Second Keeping: Pre-Dawn

The borderlands belonged neither to night nor morning.

They were the color of ash before fire. The grass grew low and silver. The trees leaned away from one another. Stones marked the boundary, half-buried, their faces furred with moss.

Beyond them was the pasture-world.

We had never held fellowship with the sheep.

That should be said.

We mocked their softness. We laughed at their terror. We called them foolish because they ran when shadows moved and huddled when thunder spoke. In the days before our fathers remembered the bond, they had hunted them.

But the bond had outlived those fathers.

I came to the last place of the veil, where the trees thinned and mist lay low against the stones.

There I found a lamb.

Or the first face of one.

It stood alone beneath a blackthorn tree, one eye clouded white, the other wide and wet with terror. Its wool was clotted with burrs and blood. Flies moved along its mouth. When it heard me, it tried to run, but its legs folded beneath it, and from its throat came the cry I had heard in the forest.

Not a bleat.

Not speech.

A breaking.

I did not yet cross.

For a moment I stood with one paw in the forest and one beside the stones.

Behind me were the dens, the mothers, the torn banners, the Hrovan.

Before me, the veil did not hide what lay beyond it.

It only made crossing a different thing than seeing.

The pasture-world did not begin.

It failed.

The flock wandered in circles beyond the veil. Some were fevered. Some had bitten themselves raw. Some pressed their heads against stones. Lambs searched for mothers who no longer knew them. Ewes gave milk darkened by rot.

Among the afflicted moved those who struck them, drove them, or fed upon whatever fell. Some had the shape of wolves. Some had the shape of sheep. Some wore a likeness that shifted whenever I tried to know it. The wounded lay among those who wounded. The frightened ran beside those who made them flee. No shape told me who had harmed, who had suffered, or who might yet cease.

I had no one name that could hold them without lying.

I crossed the stones.

The twilight deepened.

I followed the cries to a hill beneath a red predawn sky.

There I saw him.

The Shepherd stood alone.

His robe was torn. One sleeve hung dark with blood. Around his feet the earth had been trampled into mud. Creatures climbing to strike him circled below, falling and rising again. Others crawled among them, too wounded or bewildered to flee.

In his arms he carried a lamb.

Small.

White beneath the blood.

Still alive.

One scarred hand held its breast where each breath struggled to begin. The other supported its broken weight. The scars crossed his palms and remained when he moved. They had not been given on that hill.

He bore no weapon.

The Shepherd did not flee.

This angered me.

I did not understand why.

Perhaps because he looked weak. Perhaps because he looked as though he had been waiting. Perhaps because something in me wanted him to save them as lords save: by command, by terror, by force.

But he only stood there.

Holding the lamb.

He said nothing.

His face did not command.

It did not pity from above.

It did not explain why he remained.

The lamb's blood lay across his hands.

That troubled me more than if he had spoken.

Then I returned to the Hrovan.

"My lord," I said, "that which cries is not outside the Troth."

The hall was silent.

The wolves in the shadows watched me.

I told him of the flock. I told him of the fever and the rot. I told him of those who harmed and those who lay beneath them, and of the likenesses I could not hold in one shape. I told him of the hill, the red sky, the wounded human Shepherd, and the living lamb in his arms.

When I had finished, I asked for the answer I did not want to hear.

"What would you have me do, Lord?"

The Hrovan closed his eyes.

For a long time he did not move.

Then he said, "You know the bond, Vaerun."

I did.

That was the wound of it.

He opened his eyes.

"When the cry enters the Troth..."

I finished the words because I had known them longer than I had understood them.

"...no wolf may look away."

The floor seemed to fall beneath me.

"My lord."

"My son."

I hated him then, a little.

Not because he was cruel.

Because he was not.

Cruelty would have been easier to bear.

The Troth had not been given to me as a tale to master. I had first recognized its demand in silence, in the way wolves lowered their heads when the Shepherd's name was spoken, in the knowledge that a cry heard could not be made unheard.

But hearing had not told me how to answer.

The Hrovan came down from the stone seat and placed his forehead against mine.

His breath smelled of winter.

"Summon those who have not been released," he said.

He did not say Truvane.

He did not need to.

Then he lifted his head.

At first I thought he was answering the cries beyond the veil.

Then I understood.

He was carrying them.

The sound that came from him was not command only, and not grief only. It began with the lamb's cry, thin and broken, taken into the deep chamber of a wolf's chest. Then it widened beyond mourning, beyond warning, beyond speech.

The howl passed through the hall.

The torn banners stirred though no wind entered.

The wolves beneath them lowered their heads.

Beyond the stone hollow, other throats took it up: first the watchers, then the mothers, then the blind ones, then the cubs who did not yet know why they trembled.

The howl that had heard the cry crossed the land.

It moved through the forest as the cry had moved through it before dawn.

But this was not the cry.

This was the cry remembered by wolves.

This was the cry heard.

By the time it reached the boundary stones, every den had heard it. Every scar had heard it. Every wolf still bound to answer had heard itself named.

The Truvane would come.

I lowered my head.

The hall had entrusted me to cross. I still mistook that trust for sufficiency.

## The Third Keeping: The Calling of the Truvane

Before we ran toward the hill, the Truvane came.

They came because the howl had passed over the dens and left no faithful thing untouched.

They gathered beneath the torn banners in the hollow of stone, where vows had been spoken before any living wolf had a name: mothers, cubs, watchers, singers, scarred ones, lame ones, blind ones, wolves whose legs shook beneath their own weight.

No fire was lit.

No feast was prepared.

No drum was struck.

The Truvane did not come to celebrate.

They came to listen.

I stood before them with my head lowered.

The Hrovan stood behind me.

Then the Gravane came out from the shadows.

Not elders only.

Scar-bearers.

Rememberers.

Those who knew that flesh remembers what passes through it, and that neither scar nor memory proves a creature faithful.

When they entered the hollow, every wolf became still.

For the Gravane remembered the four curses.

The fang turned inward.

The eye sealed shut.

The paw washed clean of blood.

The mouth closed around the cry.

They had pressed the marks into us when we were young.

Not as ornaments.

As warnings.

The marks did not belong to one body or one people. Under accusation, the faithful might taste iron at an unwounded throat, see the world flatten behind a film of ash, feel a paw become smooth and scentless with false innocence, or carry a cry trembling behind closed teeth.

Not enough to know another creature's guilt.

Enough to name a deformation before it finished its work.

The eldest of the Gravane, blind, lifted her head and spoke in the old tongue.

"Ur va. Thar un. Veth ara."

The hollow answered:

"Aru va'en."

The words entered us as thunder enters a cub: first in the body, then in whatever understanding may follow.

Before the calling began, the Hrovan came down from the stone seat.

He stood where every gathered creature could see him.

"The cry binds us to answer," he said. "It does not tell us what answer will keep faith."

The hall held his words without relieving him of them.

Then one of the Gravane spoke from among the scarred.

"The cry has told us whom we may not abandon. It has not told us whom to strike."

The Hrovan bowed his head.

So the charge changed before it was given. We would cross to witness, to shelter, to make a way through harm, and to learn what faith required. We would not go carrying a verdict the cry itself had never spoken.

The eldest Gravane walked among those gathered.

Not choosing the pure.

Listening for the calling.

She paused before a wolf whose shoulders were thick with strength. He lifted his head too quickly. His eyes were bright. He wanted to be called.

The Gravane passed him by.

The Gravane came to Harrow, whose left ear had been torn away by a boar.

Harrow did not lift his head.

He shook.

Not much.

Only enough.

The Gravane touched her brow to his.

"Harrow."

The Truvane answered:

"Aru va'en."

So the first was called.

Sable was called next, singing under his breath because silence frightened him.

"Do not hide the song," said the Gravane.

Sable lowered himself to the ground.

"Sable."

"Aru va'en."

So the second was called.

Then Murn.

A murmur passed through the hollow.

His bones were aged. His breath was rough. One winter had nearly taken him already, and the next was waiting.

He grinned when the blind Gravane faced him.

"I wondered when you would find me," he said.

The Gravane struck him lightly across the muzzle.

"You were never hidden."

"Murn."

"Aru va'en."

So the third was called.

Beyond Murn stood another wolf.

His legs shook against the stone.

When the Gravane came to him, he did not lift his head.

She touched her brow to his.

"Not this crossing," he said.

His voice broke.

The Gravane did not withdraw the touch.

"None of us is the measure."

She did not speak his name.

Then she moved on.

The wolf remained among the Truvane, his legs still shaking.

The crossing would go without him.

The answering would not.

The fourth calling fell upon Little Vey.

He was not little anymore.

But names given in cubhood are not easily taken back, and his had gone deeper than size.

Vey was an old word.

It had meant small. Then ember. Then the last light under ash.

Among the Vaelun, we used the name gently.

The Gravane did not.

When they spoke it, they lowered their heads.

Little Vey stood beside me, trying not to tremble and failing.

When the Gravane came near, he looked first to me.

Not to the Hrovan.

Not to the banners.

To me.

"Vaerun," he whispered.

The word seemed too large in his mouth.

Or perhaps he was the only one among us small enough to carry it rightly.

The Gravane heard him.

"That is why," she said.

Then she touched his brow.

"Vey."

"Aru va'en."

So the fourth was called.

Then the others were named.

Brindle, broad-backed and gentle, who had once carried a starving cub three days through snow and never spoken of it.

Keld, hard of jaw and slow to anger, whose silence did not hide emptiness but depth.

Ash, pale as winter grass, who feared blood and came anyway.

Rook, black-pawed and sharp-eyed, who saw movement at the edge of things before others thought to look.

Yarrow, wound-licker and herb-finder, whose mercy had never been clean.

Thorn, fierce and narrow, who had spent his life learning where anger ended and guardianship began.

Ostra, scarred across the ribs, who had lost three brothers beyond the veil and still came when the cries began.

Fen, mud-colored and quiet, who knew how to stand in sinking ground.

After each name, the Truvane answered:

"Aru va'en."

With each answer, the hollow grew heavier.

Not with glory.

With recognition.

These were not the fearless.

These were not the pure.

These were not wolves who could bear the Troth without being kept.

They were creatures called into a crossing whose measure none of them possessed.

When the twelfth had been named, the Gravane turned to me.

I waited for the old words.

None came.

The Hrovan placed his muzzle near my ear.

"You were called before you entered the hollow," he said.

Twelve beside me.

Twelve Vaelun called to cross.

And I, a Vaerun, bound to the veil but not enthroned above them.

The wolf who was not ready to cross remained among the Truvane. The Gravane stood with those they had named. The Hrovan stood among us rather than above us.

Then the saying of departure began.

The Hrovan and the gathered Truvane spoke:

"Though the road divide us."

The called answered:

"Though the night conceal us."

And the whole hall, those who would remain and those who would cross, said:

"The Troth remains!"

The words belonged to no single throat.

The Hrovan's eyes passed over Harrow, Sable, Murn, Little Vey, Brindle, Keld, Ash, Rook, Yarrow, Thorn, Ostra, and Fen.

Last, they came to me.

He did not tell us to be unafraid.

He did not call our going righteous.

The departure had already said what any one voice could carry.

Little Vey stepped close behind me.

He said nothing more.

His shoulder found my flank and steadied there.

So we went.

## The Fourth Keeping: Eternal Dawn

We ran toward the hill in twilight.

Twelve beside me.

Twelve Vaelun.

No one spoke after we crossed the stones.

The veil closed behind us without sound.

The White Moon still hung at the edge of the world, thin and stricken, not yet gone from the sky.

Then the Black Sun rose against it.

It came without morning.

The twilight did not become dawn.

It became exposure.

One moment we were moving through the last gray of the borderlands. The next, every wound was visible. Every tooth. Every fly. Every smear of blood drying black in wool.

Black light from the east.

Pale light kept in the west.

Between them, the sky opened like a wound torn anew.

The Black Sun did not warm the world.

It opened it.

Every scent rose naked beneath it: blood in wool, terror in milk, fever under skin, rot sweetening at the edge of wounds, dung, wet iron, trampled grass, and the rank breath of creatures who had been made strange to themselves.

Nothing could hide its smell.

Not the lamb.

Not those who had harmed it.

Not us.

Exposure was not yet truth.

The Black Sun showed each fragment alone and called the fragment whole.

The hill stood before us as though it had been waiting inside the wound.

The Shepherd saw us coming.

He did not smile.

He did not thank us.

He looked at us as one looks at rain after drought: with grief and recognition.

Behind him, the White Moon sank lower but did not vanish.

Above him, the Black Sun climbed.

We formed the circle around him.

The first mercy was knowing where to stand.

The lamb cried once.

Then the assault came up the hill.

It broke upon us like weather.

The first body struck Harrow and tore his shoulder. Brindle and Thorn caught the assailant between them, shoulder against flank, and turned its teeth away. They held until it ceased lunging, then opened a path down the hill and let it go.

No fang entered it.

No claw opened it.

Harrow bled.

That was how the cost began.

At the center, the Shepherd began to call.

Not to us alone.

He called into the flock below, name after name, as though no scream, false voice, or Black Sun could make him forget whom he had come to gather.

While he called, he worked.

He pressed one scarred hand beneath the lamb's breast and waited through each broken breath. With the other he tore strips from his robe, washed mud from the wounds with water from a fold of cloth, and bound what had been opened. He did not hurry the creature past its pain. He did not wait for it to prove a will to live.

Blood slowed beneath his hands.

Breath ceased its drowning.

Torn flesh drew by degrees toward torn flesh.

The lamb remained wounded.

Still the Shepherd called.

Its near ear moved beneath his voice.

Later, with an effort that shook its neck, its head turned toward him.

It did not stand.

It did not answer.

It began to remember what it was.

Around him, we made room between bodies as he made room within them.

Rook sent Fen into the falling press. Keld and Murn bore a wolf-shaped assailant to earth. When its limbs stopped striking, they rolled away and left the hill open beneath it.

Claws entered us.

Teeth found us.

Fever passed across torn skin. The assailants screamed with voices almost like ours. Some wore the shoulders of wolves, some the faces of sheep, some a likeness that shifted with the eye that watched it. Some we sheltered struck when touched. Some we held collapsed when their rage had passed.

An assailant broke Keld's hold and came for the lamb. I met it with my shoulder and threw it down the slope. It rolled, found its feet, and stopped beyond the reach of my body.

Still I followed.

One step.

Then another.

The back of its neck opened before my sight. I wanted the ending my teeth could give.

Thorn struck my ribs from the side and set his body across my path.

I turned on him.

At the center, the Shepherd's face changed.

He did not command me back.

His grief grew.

His grief had room for every creature on the hill without calling the harm good.

The lamb felt it and shuddered beneath his hands.

In that grief I saw protection changing its face within me. The Black Sun offered the first blow as warrant for the next.

I returned to my place.

Thorn returned with me.

The Shepherd bent again over the lamb.

Then the fallen came among the assailants.

Not apart from the assault.

Within it.

They wore voices we loved.

A brother's laugh, almost right.

A mother's call, almost tender.

The face of a dead companion held a breath too long, as though something had learned the shape but not the sorrow.

Harrow's laugh came from a mouth that did not smell of Harrow.

Sable's song came from a throat without Sable's trembling in it.

Little Vey's face appeared below us without the warm scent of ash and rain that belonged to him.

One wore my eyes and smelled of a den I had never entered.

Each likeness was wrong by less than sight could name.

Not enough to pronounce a verdict.

Enough to cause the blood to hesitate.

They did not merely lie.

They accused with what was true.

One called Harrow steadfast and showed him every time steadfastness had become refusal to receive help.

One sang with Sable's hidden voice and showed him every silence in which he had withheld the song another needed.

One wore Murn's younger face and told him that age had made every burden he accepted into a burden placed upon others.

One looked through my eyes and showed me how quickly I had desired pursuit.

The acts were not invented.

The verdict was.

This is all you are.

This is all you have ever been.

This is all you can become.

The four curses passed over the hill like shadows over water.

Harrow's fang drew inward until the point rested against his own throat. Far below, a dry film crossed the eye of a sheep beneath the press. Ash's bloodied paw became smooth beneath him, clean of scent and weight. Sable's breath struck a closure behind his teeth, and the song shook there without passage.

The marks crossed faithful and fallen alike.

At the center, the Shepherd did not sort clean bodies from deformed ones.

He sorted within them.

He laid two fingers against Harrow's jaw and drew the fang away from his throat, turning its point toward the open boundary where another blow might come. He took Ash's smooth paw in his scarred hand and pressed it into mud, wool, pulse, and the weight of another creature needing help. He touched Sable beneath the jaw until the trapped breath passed his teeth as one broken note.

He cut nothing away.

He returned each part within the circle to its body and each body toward relation beneath him.

Then he called again.

Far down the hill, the sheep beneath the ash-dry film heard.

The Shepherd had spoken a name I did not know. In the press below, one ear turned toward him. The sheep's body followed by less than a paw's width.

From the sheep came a cry too soft for the rest of us to hear.

Little Vey heard it.

Then its scent reached him: fever, ash, harm done and received, and beneath them earth opening under rain.

Vey smelled remembrance.

He said nothing. He slipped behind the bodies holding the lower edge, crossed beneath Brindle's bent stance, and sought me. At my wounded side, he set his head below my shoulder and turned toward the movement he had found.

His weight steadied mine.

I followed his attention. Rook followed mine. Then the others saw.

The sheep below us had been driving a smaller creature uphill beneath its chest. At another command from the shifting host, it lowered its head to strike.

The Shepherd called its name again.

The sheep stopped. Its body shook. The command came again from behind it.

The sheep turned sideways instead.

It took the blow meant for the smaller creature, then lifted one foreleg and made an opening beneath itself.

The smaller creature crawled through.

Our circle opened.

Fen and Brindle drew apart while Keld and Thorn held the press.

It passed first.

The sheep followed.

No fang closed. Its passage erased no harm, and no wounded creature was asked to trust it.

The Shepherd made room beside the lamb and received the smaller creature. Then he turned to the sheep.

The ash-colored film over its eye had hardened like wax. He worked his thumb beneath its edge and drew it away, opening the eye beneath it.

The sheep flinched at the first depth of the world.

The first thing it saw clearly was the wolves holding the passage.

It saw our teeth and saw that they did not close. It saw our bodies holding back harm while leaving a way through. Its breath changed. Fang had returned to its proper place.

Then it looked beyond us to the Shepherd for whom the passage had been held.

The hill saw with it. Some began to turn. Some withdrew. Some climbed harder, as though an opened way were an accusation they could not bear.

Return had become visible.

It had not become easy.

Little Vey was moving back toward his place when a flailing claw caught one ear and tore it along the outer fold.

He fell, rose, and leaned toward his better side to hear.

Yarrow bound the ear while Vey pressed close beneath my shoulder again.

The assault tightened.

Harrow took a second wound beneath the first. Brindle and Rook carried him inward before the next body fell. Yarrow pressed herbs and cloth into the opening. Murn lay along Harrow's back to keep him warm. The circle closed against the blow and opened to their need.

Harrow died there.

We covered him enough to keep mud from his face, and his place among the living remained empty.

Murn rose from beside him with ribs damaged and breath roughened. He was still Murn. Sable's voice broke when he tried to sing, but a broken song passed where silence had sealed him. Brindle's back bent beneath a falling body and did not straighten. Blood ran along my side from rib toward shoulder.

Still the Shepherd called.

Still he tended.

The lamb's breathing strengthened. Its head no longer fell away from his voice. When a body broke against the inner ring and jarred the Shepherd to one knee, the lamb's bound wound opened and blood warmed his palm again.

The Shepherd gathered it closer.

His grief deepened.

We felt its room at our backs. The circle yielded beneath the blow, widened around the fallen, and held.

Under the Black Sun, the ordeal seemed to outlast every hour by which creatures measure night. Yet the White Moon remained at the edge, thin as mercy and just as easily overlooked.

Then came the wish.

Small.

Shameful.

Merciful, it almost seemed.

That the lamb would stop crying.

Not die. Only fall silent, and end the tearing inside me.

The Black Sun found the truth in that wish.

It showed me every cry I had answered because I wanted to be the one who answered. It showed me the stone seat I claimed not to desire. It showed me my teeth at the boundary and the pleasure of one step taken too far.

Then it pronounced the lie.

You have never wanted the cry.

You have wanted only to be named faithful for hearing it.

My paw lost the smell of blood.

Mud became smooth beneath it.

My mouth closed gently around the lamb's sound.

Not biting.

Closing.

The hill became clean.

No wool.

No milk.

No blood.

No small life breathing against the Shepherd's torn robe.

I began to fall inward, into a silence warm as a den and without any cry.

At my side, Little Vey shifted his weight beneath mine.

He did not tell me what I was becoming.

He simply remained where I could feel him.

Across the clean emptiness, the Shepherd's grief met me.

Not accusation.

Not permission.

Grief for the cry I wanted ended, and for the creature in me closing its mouth around it.

The lamb turned its wounded head toward his voice.

One breath entered it.

Then another.

The world regained its smell.

Blood.

Mud.

Wool.

Vey's torn ear bound beside me.

My falsely clean paw sank into the earth beneath his weight. The trapped cry passed my teeth as breath.

I opened a passage.

I held.

The circle had lost Harrow.

His place remained empty.

Beneath the Shepherd, the living held one another.

The Black Sun pressed lower.

The hill filled with cries: the wounded, the returning, those enraged by return, and those who had forgotten which cry was theirs. The Shepherd answered with names.

No blow broke the Black Sun.

No death purchased morning.

Beneath his calling, the Black Sun's final sentence began to crack.

The assault did not.

It came once more with all the weight left in it.

Fen sank beneath Rook. Ash and Yarrow carried the wounded inward. Thorn threw back a body, then returned to the opening. Sable gave one torn note, and the circle shifted around it.

Brindle's legs folded.

The inner ring opened around him.

Through that opening an assailant reached the Shepherd.

I entered between them.

The strike opened my side.

I threw the body only far enough for Thorn and Keld to take its movement from it. Then my forelegs failed.

I did not rise.

The ground received me.

At the center, the Shepherd lowered the lamb beside his knee without releasing it from his care.

One of the host struck him, its mouth closed around its own cry.

He opened both hands and gave ground beneath its weight.

He did not throw it back.

The lamb stood.

Its legs shook.

Blood still marked the binding around its body.

It looked toward the Shepherd.

Then the press of bodies cut the few steps between the lamb and the Shepherd.

I lay across that narrow crossing, my jaws open with exhaustion.

The lamb came to me.

No one placed it in my mouth.

It stepped between my teeth and chose the shelter there.

I closed upon wool and nothing more.

Fang became cradle.

With the strength left in me, I carried the lamb beyond the falling bodies and into the room the circle had made.

One step.

Then none.

The lamb remained warm between my jaws.

Darkness received me.

## The Fifth Keeping: Mourn

Where no companion could follow and no voice could answer for me, I was given a thing I have spoken only in this Keeping.

Not because it was hidden.

Because it was too large for ordinary speech.

I do not know whether the others saw it.

Some visions are not given to the worthy.

They enter at the breaking place, where breaking has made room.

I saw the hollow of stone beneath the torn banners, though no roof stood over it and no walls enclosed it.

I saw the stone seat.

Empty.

Not abandoned.

Behind it lay a wolf vast enough to shelter the hollow as mountains shelter a valley.

It did not crouch to spring.

It did not open its jaws to devour.

Its body had been gathered from keeping: memory beside memory, wound beside wound, vows borne with failure, responsibility received and passed on. No life within it had ceased to be itself.

Aruvan.

The long wolf made by keeping.

Not by conquest.

Not by hunger.

Not by an office preserving itself.

I saw the Hrovan I had known lay down what the hall had entrusted. Vaelun kept thresholds open. Vaerun crossed with those who could not cross alone. Gravane returned forgotten names to the wounded. Truvane took one another's weight.

I had thought Aruvan was the greatest body I would ever see.

Then it lowered its head.

Not to me.

To the one before it.

There stood the Shepherd.

A man.

Wounded.

Breathing.

His robe torn.

His human hands scarred and open.

The Black Sun had called grief the final name of the world. The Shepherd bore grief without making it final. Love had come first; only the beloved could be grieved.

Aruvan bowed lower.

The ribs that had enclosed generations opened without breaking. Names, memories, warnings, skills, debts, and unfinished responsibilities passed outward like breath returned to living lungs.

Nothing faithful was lost.

Nothing carried long remained possessed.

Through the ribs came the flock.

Not pale figures.

Not obedient shadows.

Living creatures, each in its own body. The lamb I had carried was among them and remained the lamb: wounded, breathing, choosing its steps. Others gathered near it without dissolving into it.

I saw then one communion under two forms.

The living flock moved through the open ribs toward the Shepherd.

He received them without closing his hands around them.

The vast shape became breath, warmth, and memory returned to those responsible to bear it. No ancestor or hidden hall remained behind the world.

The Shepherd remained.

Not by force.

Not by command.

By the fidelity no creature had made and no failure could exhaust.

The vision receded, drawing the hollow away.

The torn banners folded into darkness.

Someone was calling me back.

Not loudly.

Not by command.

By morning.

## Epilogue

When I opened my eyes, the hill remained.

Mud held the print of every body that had crossed it. Blood darkened the grass. The wounded lay where they had been carried. Harrow's body rested beneath the covering we had drawn over him. The air smelled of opened flesh, trampled roots, damp wool, and the first water moving again beneath the stones.

The Black Sun no longer held the east.

An ordinary sun rose there.

The White Moon faded at the other rim, its pale light not destroyed but gathered into morning.

The ground was this wounded world, still capable of bearing what had been promised to it.

Grass stood where it had not been torn away. Dew lay beside blood without confusing the two. Wolf, sheep, mud, milk, herbs, and running water returned to their own scents.

The lamb was still between my jaws.

I opened them.

It stepped down by its own strength.

The binding remained around its body. One wound still seeped where the struggle had opened it again. Its legs trembled, but it did not fall.

It looked first toward the Shepherd.

Then it waited.

The sheep whose eye he had opened stood a short distance away. It remained marked by what it had done and by what had been done to it.

The lamb went to it.

The sheep lowered its opened eye. The lamb stood beside it. Together they walked toward the Shepherd.

The flock began to follow at different distances.

Some came near.

Some remained beyond the reach of any fang.

Some had withdrawn before morning and did not return.

The Shepherd received those who came and held no offense against those who did not.

The veil thinned with the coming of the sun. Gravane crossed first.

With them, we counted the living.

We counted again.

We named the missing.

We tended the wounds that could be tended. Yarrow cleaned my side and left the opening to close in flesh's time. Murn's damaged ribs made every breath a rough labor. Sable's voice returned in fragments and broke when he tested it. Little Vey leaned his uninjured ear toward whoever spoke and missed some words on the torn side. Brindle rose beneath a back that would remain bent and painful.

Other hurts were tended and left unnamed.

Among the whole company, Gravane perceived something scars could not measure.

Vethra.

It was borne by every companion who had passed through the Keeping, wounded and unwounded, living and dead. It did not shine or take one shape. It made familiar flesh seem almost otherwise, but not with the wrongness the fallen had worn.

Coal beneath ash.

First light before fullness.

Fidelity considered and borne, not payment or proof.

Its measure belonged to the Shepherd.

The Gravane paused over only a few.

They came last to Harrow, named him among the dead, and covered him. Their hands lingered at his brow; the first light was borne even there, though no breath returned.

No answer came from beneath the cloth.

No answer was demanded.

When the dead were named, grief opened Ostra's stance until another mourner could stand beside her. Her scar remained at her ribs. Her three brothers did not return with morning.

Vethra was borne and left unclaimed.

After the missing had been counted, the wounded tended, and the dead covered, the defensive circle gave way to an open ring.

We made passages through it around the Shepherd. There was no head place and no seat from the hall.

Harrow's absence remained among us as an unfilled space.

We placed no food there.

Then the Hrovan and the Truvane came on foot through the opened veil.

They brought bread and water.

No banners came with them.

The Hrovan entered the ring, set the provision upon the ground, and stood beside it. Then he spoke:

> I sent you beneath a charge I had not understood.
>
> The stone seat entrusted me to answer.
>
> It did not make my answer faithful.

The gathering answered with silence.

I did not absolve him.

The Hrovan waited beside the bread and water.

The Shepherd came to the provision.

His hands were the same hands I had seen beneath the Black Sun.

Scarred.

Human.

Still marked with the lamb's blood beneath the washing of morning.

He received what the Hrovan had brought, gave thanks, broke the bread, and opened his hands.

The provision became common.

The Hrovan poured water first for the wounded. He drank only after another vessel had passed from his hands.

The Gravane spoke the names of the dead as they carried water to the living.

The Truvane carried bread and water through every passage. The wolf who had said, "Not this crossing," served among them.

The Vaelun changed places at the passages so each could eat.

I took bread across the line where the circle had first closed and carried it to the flock and those beginning return.

When each had received what could be received, I came back among my companions.

I did not preside.

The wounded lamb walked beside the returning sheep into the open ring. The flock approached without being herded. Some ate close to the Shepherd. Some took bread and withdrew. Fang and fleece shared hunger and remained themselves.

No lamb was killed for the meal.

Morning was not the Feast.

It was bread eaten in its promise.

Vethra remained nearly hidden as bread passed from one creature to another.

No seat, vessel, or portion was set for the Troth.

The meal quieted.

The lamb rested against the Shepherd. Its wounds remained bound. Its breath rose and fell beneath one scarred hand.

Little Vey came and sat beside me.

He did not draw the eye.

Only when my wounded side began to fail did he move beneath it, bearing enough of my weight that I did not stand alone.

We watched the Shepherd together.

Vey spoke once.

"Before the Troth, there was the Shepherd."

Before the vow was the cry.

Before grief was love.

We do not keep alone.

## Spelling and Pronunciation Guide

**Aru va'en** — AH-roo VAH-en

A keeping of the Troth; the response of the faithful at the calling.

**Aruvan** — ah-ROO-vahn

The long wolf made by keeping; the gathered body of faithful memory, responsibility, and practice across generations, relinquished beneath the Shepherd rather than preserved as an autonomous sacred body.

**Gravane** — grah-VAH-neh

Scar-bearers and rememberers; those who carry and press the old signs, return forgotten names to the wounded, and perceive Vethra fallibly.

**Hrovan** — HROH-vahn

The one who bears the hall; the lord or center-bearer entrusted with responsibility for the people. The office does not certify righteousness.

**Truvane** — troo-VAH-neh

The gathered faithful; those who have not been released from the Troth.

**Vaelun** — VAY-loon

Threshold-keepers; those set at the border between worlds.

**Vaerun** — VAY-roon

The office of crossing; the one bound to pass the veil with those who cannot cross alone.

**Vethra** — VETH-rah

First light beneath ash; the subtle testimony borne upon flesh through which fidelity has passed. It is not a wound, credential, payment, curse-mark, or institutional sign.

**Vey** — VAY

An old word: small, then ember, then the last light under ash.

**Ur va. Thar un. Veth ara.** — OOR VAH. THAHR OON. VETH AH-rah.

The old tongue of the calling; literal meaning lost, understood by trembling.
