## The Third Keeping: The Calling of the Truvane

Before we ran toward the hill, the Truvane came.

They came because the howl had passed over the dens and left no faithful thing untouched.

They gathered beneath the torn banners in the hollow of stone, where vows had been spoken before any living wolf had a name: mothers, cubs, watchers, singers, scarred ones, lame ones, blind ones, wolves whose legs shook beneath their own weight.

No fire was lit.

No feast was prepared.

No drum was struck.

The Truvane did not come to celebrate.

They came to listen.

I stood before them with my head lowered.

The Hrovan stood behind me.

Then the Gravane came out from the shadows.

Not elders only.

Scar-bearers.

Rememberers.

Those who knew that flesh remembers what passes through it, and that neither scar nor memory proves a creature faithful.

When they entered the hollow, every wolf became still.

For the Gravane remembered the four curses.

The fang turned inward.

The eye sealed shut.

The paw washed clean of blood.

The mouth closed around the cry.

They had pressed the marks into us when we were young.

Not as ornaments.

As warnings.

The marks did not belong to one body or one people. Under accusation, the faithful might taste iron at an unwounded throat, see the world flatten behind a film of ash, feel a paw become smooth and scentless with false innocence, or carry a cry trembling behind closed teeth.

Not enough to know another creature's guilt.

Enough to name a deformation before it finished its work.

The eldest of the Gravane, blind, lifted her head and spoke in the old tongue.

"Ur va. Thar un. Veth ara."

The hollow answered:

"Aru va'en."

The words entered us as thunder enters a cub: first in the body, then in whatever understanding may follow.

Before the calling began, the Hrovan came down from the stone seat.

He stood where every gathered creature could see him.

"The cry binds us to answer," he said. "It does not tell us what answer will keep faith."

The hall held his words without relieving him of them.

Then one of the Gravane spoke from among the scarred.

"The cry has told us whom we may not abandon. It has not told us whom to strike."

The Hrovan bowed his head.

So the charge changed before it was given. We would cross to witness, to shelter, to make a way through harm, and to learn what faith required. We would not go carrying a verdict the cry itself had never spoken.

The eldest Gravane walked among those gathered.

Not choosing the pure.

Listening for the calling.

She paused before a wolf whose shoulders were thick with strength. He lifted his head too quickly. His eyes were bright. He wanted to be called.

The Gravane passed him by.

The Gravane came to Harrow, whose left ear had been torn away by a boar.

Harrow did not lift his head.

He shook.

Not much.

Only enough.

The Gravane touched her brow to his.

"Harrow."

The Truvane answered:

"Aru va'en."

So the first was called.

Sable was called next, singing under his breath because silence frightened him.

"Do not hide the song," said the Gravane.

Sable lowered himself to the ground.

"Sable."

"Aru va'en."

So the second was called.

Then Murn.

A murmur passed through the hollow.

His bones were aged. His breath was rough. One winter had nearly taken him already, and the next was waiting.

He grinned when the blind Gravane faced him.

"I wondered when you would find me," he said.

The Gravane struck him lightly across the muzzle.

"You were never hidden."

"Murn."

"Aru va'en."

So the third was called.

Beyond Murn stood another wolf.

His legs shook against the stone.

When the Gravane came to him, he did not lift his head.

She touched her brow to his.

"Not this crossing," he said.

His voice broke.

The Gravane did not withdraw the touch.

"None of us is the measure."

She did not speak his name.

Then she moved on.

The wolf remained among the Truvane, his legs still shaking.

The crossing would go without him.

The answering would not.

The fourth calling fell upon Little Vey.

He was not little anymore.

But names given in cubhood are not easily taken back, and his had gone deeper than size.

Vey was an old word.

It had meant small. Then ember. Then the last light under ash.

Among the Vaelun, we used the name gently.

The Gravane did not.

When they spoke it, they lowered their heads.

Little Vey stood beside me, trying not to tremble and failing.

When the Gravane came near, he looked first to me.

Not to the Hrovan.

Not to the banners.

To me.

"Vaerun," he whispered.

The word seemed too large in his mouth.

Or perhaps he was the only one among us small enough to carry it rightly.

The Gravane heard him.

"That is why," she said.

Then she touched his brow.

"Vey."

"Aru va'en."

So the fourth was called.

Then the others were named.

Brindle, broad-backed and gentle, who had once carried a starving cub three days through snow and never spoken of it.

Keld, hard of jaw and slow to anger, whose silence did not hide emptiness but depth.

Ash, pale as winter grass, who feared blood and came anyway.

Rook, black-pawed and sharp-eyed, who saw movement at the edge of things before others thought to look.

Yarrow, wound-licker and herb-finder, whose mercy had never been clean.

Thorn, fierce and narrow, who had spent his life learning where anger ended and guardianship began.

Ostra, scarred across the ribs, who had lost three brothers beyond the veil and still came when the cries began.

Fen, mud-colored and quiet, who knew how to stand in sinking ground.

After each name, the Truvane answered:

"Aru va'en."

With each answer, the hollow grew heavier.

Not with glory.

With recognition.

These were not the fearless.

These were not the pure.

These were not wolves who could bear the Troth without being kept.

They were creatures called into a crossing whose measure none of them possessed.

When the twelfth had been named, the Gravane turned to me.

I waited for the old words.

None came.

The Hrovan placed his muzzle near my ear.

"You were called before you entered the hollow," he said.

Twelve beside me.

Twelve Vaelun called to cross.

And I, a Vaerun, bound to the veil but not enthroned above them.

The wolf who was not ready to cross remained among the Truvane. The Gravane stood with those they had named. The Hrovan stood among us rather than above us.

Then the saying of departure began.

The Hrovan and the gathered Truvane spoke:

"Though the road divide us."

The called answered:

"Though the night conceal us."

And the whole hall, those who would remain and those who would cross, said:

"The Troth remains!"

The words belonged to no single throat.

The Hrovan's eyes passed over Harrow, Sable, Murn, Little Vey, Brindle, Keld, Ash, Rook, Yarrow, Thorn, Ostra, and Fen.

Last, they came to me.

He did not tell us to be unafraid.

He did not call our going righteous.

The departure had already said what any one voice could carry.

Little Vey stepped close behind me.

He said nothing more.

His shoulder found my flank and steadied there.

So we went.
