## The Second Keeping: Pre-Dawn

The borderlands belonged neither to night nor morning.

They were the color of ash before fire. The grass grew low and silver. The trees leaned away from one another. Stones marked the boundary, half-buried, their faces furred with moss.

Beyond them was the pasture-world.

We had never held fellowship with the sheep.

That should be said.

We mocked their softness. We laughed at their terror. We called them foolish because they ran when shadows moved and huddled when thunder spoke. In the days before our fathers remembered the bond, they had hunted them.

But the bond had outlived those fathers.

I came to the last place of the veil, where the trees thinned and mist lay low against the stones.

There I found a lamb.

Or the first face of one.

It stood alone beneath a blackthorn tree, one eye clouded white, the other wide and wet with terror. Its wool was clotted with burrs and blood. Flies moved along its mouth. When it heard me, it tried to run, but its legs folded beneath it, and from its throat came the cry I had heard in the forest.

Not a bleat.

Not speech.

A breaking.

I did not yet cross.

For a moment I stood with one paw in the forest and one beside the stones.

Behind me were the dens, the mothers, the torn banners, the Hrovan.

Before me, the veil did not hide what lay beyond it.

It only made crossing a different thing than seeing.

The pasture-world did not begin.

It failed.

The flock wandered in circles beyond the veil. Some were fevered. Some had bitten themselves raw. Some pressed their heads against stones. Lambs searched for mothers who no longer knew them. Ewes gave milk darkened by rot.

Among the afflicted moved those who struck them, drove them, or fed upon whatever fell. Some had the shape of wolves. Some had the shape of sheep. Some wore a likeness that shifted whenever I tried to know it. The wounded lay among those who wounded. The frightened ran beside those who made them flee. No shape told me who had harmed, who had suffered, or who might yet cease.

I had no one name that could hold them without lying.

I crossed the stones.

The twilight deepened.

I followed the cries to a hill beneath a red predawn sky.

There I saw him.

The Shepherd stood alone.

His robe was torn. One sleeve hung dark with blood. Around his feet the earth had been trampled into mud. Creatures climbing to strike him circled below, falling and rising again. Others crawled among them, too wounded or bewildered to flee.

In his arms he carried a lamb.

Small.

White beneath the blood.

Still alive.

One scarred hand held its breast where each breath struggled to begin. The other supported its broken weight. The scars crossed his palms and remained when he moved. They had not been given on that hill.

He bore no weapon.

The Shepherd did not flee.

This angered me.

I did not understand why.

Perhaps because he looked weak. Perhaps because he looked as though he had been waiting. Perhaps because something in me wanted him to save them as lords save: by command, by terror, by force.

But he only stood there.

Holding the lamb.

He said nothing.

His face did not command.

It did not pity from above.

It did not explain why he remained.

The lamb's blood lay across his hands.

That troubled me more than if he had spoken.

Then I returned to the Hrovan.

"My lord," I said, "that which cries is not outside the Troth."

The hall was silent.

The wolves in the shadows watched me.

I told him of the flock. I told him of the fever and the rot. I told him of those who harmed and those who lay beneath them, and of the likenesses I could not hold in one shape. I told him of the hill, the red sky, the wounded human Shepherd, and the living lamb in his arms.

When I had finished, I asked for the answer I did not want to hear.

"What would you have me do, Lord?"

The Hrovan closed his eyes.

For a long time he did not move.

Then he said, "You know the bond, Vaerun."

I did.

That was the wound of it.

He opened his eyes.

"When the cry enters the Troth..."

I finished the words because I had known them longer than I had understood them.

"...no wolf may look away."

The floor seemed to fall beneath me.

"My lord."

"My son."

I hated him then, a little.

Not because he was cruel.

Because he was not.

Cruelty would have been easier to bear.

The Troth had not been given to me as a tale to master. I had first recognized its demand in silence, in the way wolves lowered their heads when the Shepherd's name was spoken, in the knowledge that a cry heard could not be made unheard.

But hearing had not told me how to answer.

The Hrovan came down from the stone seat and placed his forehead against mine.

His breath smelled of winter.

"Summon those who have not been released," he said.

He did not say Truvane.

He did not need to.

Then he lifted his head.

At first I thought he was answering the cries beyond the veil.

Then I understood.

He was carrying them.

The sound that came from him was not command only, and not grief only. It began with the lamb's cry, thin and broken, taken into the deep chamber of a wolf's chest. Then it widened beyond mourning, beyond warning, beyond speech.

The howl passed through the hall.

The torn banners stirred though no wind entered.

The wolves beneath them lowered their heads.

Beyond the stone hollow, other throats took it up: first the watchers, then the mothers, then the blind ones, then the cubs who did not yet know why they trembled.

The howl that had heard the cry crossed the land.

It moved through the forest as the cry had moved through it before dawn.

But this was not the cry.

This was the cry remembered by wolves.

This was the cry heard.

By the time it reached the boundary stones, every den had heard it. Every scar had heard it. Every wolf still bound to answer had heard itself named.

The Truvane would come.

I lowered my head.

The hall had entrusted me to cross. I still mistook that trust for sufficiency.
