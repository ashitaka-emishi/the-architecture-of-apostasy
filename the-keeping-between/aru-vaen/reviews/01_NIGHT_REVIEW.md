# Aru va'en

## A Keeping of the Troth

Before throne and pasture, before fang and fleece, there was the Troth.  
Before the Troth had a name, it had an ache.

## The First Keeping: Night

Cries in the dark.

At first I thought they were wolves.

Then I thought they were children.

Then I understood that some cries are older than names. They come up from the torn places of the world, from fields where blood has entered the soil, from mouths that no longer know whether they are praying or dying.

They came before dawn because before dawn is when the forest listens.

The dens grew restless. Mothers drew their young closer. Wolves who had lived through many winters lifted their heads but did not rise. Even the blind ones opened their eyes toward the border, toward that thin place where the trees ended and the mist began.

I was young then, though not as young as I wanted to be.

I am Vaelun.

A scout of the outer ring. A watcher of the boundary stones. One who keeps to the places where our scent thins and other things begin.

Yet the Vaelun belonged to more than the outer ring.

We stood at thresholds: of flesh, of grief, of the world's torn places.

Vaelun.

Brother, watcher, coal-under-ash.

So the Gravane remembered us.

Among the Vaelun I bore the office of Vaerun.

A Vaelun watched the veil. A Vaerun crossed when and where it must be crossed.

Others had borne that burden before me, and others might bear it after. There was little majesty in the office. Mostly it was mud, hunger, cold, and listening.

Still, the office mattered.

Its charge came before the hall's command.

The trust had already bound me.

Then the cries came again.

They moved through the forest like waves of sickness. The air tasted of acid and copper. Somewhere beyond the veil, something living dragged itself across leaves. Something wounded called out and was answered by something worse.

I did not want to go.

This is the first truth.

Not that I went bravely.

Not that I was eager.

I knew that answering the cry would require more than crossing a line of stones. It would ask something costly, and I could not know beforehand what faithfulness would demand.

The hall had entrusted me with the crossing. I feared the crossing would require the whole of that trust and find me wanting.

I trembled when I entered the hall. I bowed beneath the torn banners and waited before the stone seat where the Hrovan watched me, his muzzle silvered, his eyes dark with remembered winters.

The Hrovan's place was the hall, as the Vaelun's was the veil and the Vaerun's the crossing.

He bore the center when the people might otherwise forget that they were one body.

We called him lord because that was easier.

The Gravane called him Hrovan and lowered their heads, not because the seat made him righteous, but because the burden was real.

He wore no crown.

The stone, the banners, and the silence were weight enough.

"Vaerun, you have heard the cry," he said.

"Yes, my lord."

"What have you discerned?"

I had heard an ache in the cry, but I could not yet say what it required.

I did not answer.

The Hrovan rose and looked toward the names beneath the banners. They belonged to wolves who had gone beyond the veil and not returned, and to others who had returned unable to speak of what they had seen.

I had touched those names as a cub. I had thought them beautiful.

Now they looked like teeth.

Some had watched the veil. Some had crossed it. Some had borne the hall. These burdens did not ascend one into another, and none promised that its bearer had answered well.

The Hrovan turned back to me.

"Go," he said. "Seek out that which cries."

The stone seat entrusted him to answer.

It did not tell either of us whether his answer would keep faith.

So I went.
