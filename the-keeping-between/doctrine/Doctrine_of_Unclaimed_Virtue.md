# The Doctrine of Unclaimed Virtue

**Status:** working synthesis of established project claims; not yet a formally ratified confessional text.

## 1. Thesis

Virtue is real. The Good is real. Christ is not morally indeterminate; Christ is the measure by which Christian claims about righteousness are judged.

The doctrine concerns a different problem: **human beings can mistake their moral self-conception for possession of the Good itself.**

Therefore the disciple does not begin from:

> I am virtuous; therefore my judgment is righteous.

The disciple begins from:

> Christ is the measure. I am accountable. My self-knowledge is partial. I must remain correctable.

Unclaimed Virtue is thus an epistemic, spiritual, and institutional discipline against **self-certifying righteousness**.

## 2. What the doctrine does not mean

It does not mean:
- good and evil are relative;
- no action can be judged;
- victims and aggressors are morally interchangeable;
- violence may never be restrained;
- conviction is pride;
- humility requires indecision;
- repentance means endless self-condemnation;
- authority is impossible;
- Christ's moral claims are merely one perspective among others.

The doctrine may require a firm judgment while refusing to convert that judgment into a declaration of the judge's moral purity.

## 3. The basic distinction

A person can truthfully say:

> This act is evil.

without concluding:

> Because I correctly named the evil, I am therefore good.

A community can say:

> We must restrain this harm.

without concluding:

> Whatever we do in opposition to that harm is therefore righteous.

This is the logic behind **before fang**: the evil of the wolf does not sanctify the fang used against it.

In compact mythic form:

> Do not go because fear has left you.<br>
> Go because the cry must be answered.<br>
> Do not bare the fang because you have been entrusted with keeping.<br>
> Let even the fang remain under judgment.

This preserves both sides of the doctrine: fear does not decide faithfulness, and entrusted responsibility does not make force self-justifying.

## 4. Fleece and victimhood

The doctrine likewise refuses to make suffering a source of moral infallibility.

The wound must be heard. The victim must not be erased, instrumentalized, or treated as expendable. But suffering does not transform a creature into the source of the Good.

Thus:

> The fang is not the measure.  
> The fleece is not the measure.  
> Christ is the measure.

This is not false equivalence. It is a refusal to derive moral sovereignty either from power or from suffering.

## 5. The Troth as objective anchor

Without an objective Good, “do not claim virtue” could collapse into skepticism. The Troth prevents that collapse.

**The Troth remains** means that the truth by which persons and communities are judged does not become false because:
- the faithful fail;
- the institution apostasizes;
- the victim becomes vengeful;
- the aggressor repents;
- the leader is wrong;
- the fellowship misunderstands itself.

The Troth is not generated by our success in keeping it.

## 6. Repentance

Unclaimed Virtue makes meaningful repentance possible.

The confession:

> **I did not remain.**

names real failure.

The answer:

> **You are not the measure.**

refuses two opposite errors:
1. self-absolution — “my failure did not matter”; and
2. self-deification through condemnation — “my failure now defines reality and makes return impossible.”

The shared confession:

> **The Troth remains.**

means that return is possible because the Good survived the failure.

Repentance therefore includes truth, responsibility, restitution where possible, changed practice, and renewed participation. Mercy must not become a lie.

## 7. The cry as diagnostic

A central practical question is:

> **What cry have I trained myself not to hear?**

Self-certifying systems often preserve righteousness by filtering out inconvenient persons and evidence. The cry exposes that closure.

But the cry is not itself an autonomous moral oracle. Competing cries exist; deception exists; coercive emotional claims exist. Therefore hearing must remain joined to discernment, truth, evidence, mercy, and Christological judgment.

## 8. Authority

Unclaimed Virtue does not abolish authority. It removes the claim that authority proves righteousness.

A leader may say:

> I have been entrusted with this decision. I have considered it. We must act. I may still be wrong.

The capacity to confess error is not necessarily a failure of authority. Often it is evidence that authority has not become idolatrous.

## 9. Institutional form

The doctrine applies to communities as strongly as to individuals.

A fellowship becomes spiritually dangerous when it argues:

> We keep the Troth; therefore our acts are righteous.

The proper grammar is:

> We attempt to keep. We remain under judgment. The Troth is not ours.

Therefore the Host may fail. A keeper may require keeping. An elder may require correction. A stranger may hear a cry the community has suppressed.

## 10. Relation to Aru Va'en

*Aru Va'en* dramatizes the doctrine through unstable identities:
- wolf and sheep;
- protector and predator;
- faithful and fallen;
- wounded and dangerous;
- leader and servant.

The mature myth should not resolve into “the virtuous Host defeats evil wolves.” It should show how false identity is resisted without making the resistor the new measure, and how persons can return when the false logos loosens its hold.

## 11. Compact doctrinal form

> **Christ is the measure.**  
> **We do not possess the Good by naming ourselves good.**  
> **We may judge acts without canonizing ourselves.**  
> **We hear the cry without making the cry an idol.**  
> **We confess failure without making failure final.**  
> **We accept authority without confusing authority with righteousness.**  
> **We return when corrected.**  
> **The Troth remains.**
