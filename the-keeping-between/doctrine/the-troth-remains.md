# The Troth Remains
## Fidelity Before Throne, Pasture, Fang, and Fleece

### Abstract

Within the theology of *Aru Va'en*, the Troth names the fidelity of God disclosed in Christ and the right relation into which creatures are called. That fidelity is prior to every created office, institution, identity, and claim to righteousness. It is not reducible to loyalty, obedience, covenantal belonging, tradition, sincerity, or perseverance. Each of these may participate in the Troth, but each may also become its counterfeit. Loyalty can serve an idol. Obedience can serve a false shepherd. Tradition can preserve apostasy. Perseverance can harden into refusal to repent.

The Troth therefore cannot be identified with the mere continuation of commitment. It names the right ordering of relation beneath the judgment of God and, within the Christological grammar of *Aru Va'en*, under the keeping of the Shepherd.

This distinction gives meaning to the central confession:

> **Before throne and pasture.  
> Before fang and fleece.  
> The Troth remains.**

The throne does not possess the Troth. The pasture does not define it. The fang cannot enforce it. The fleece cannot claim it through innocence or suffering. None is the measure; all four stand before the Shepherd's judgment.

The Troth remains because God's fidelity in Christ is not exhausted by the roles through which creatures attempt to participate in it. For this reason, institutions may fall without exhausting fidelity, guardians may repent without ceasing to have been guardians, the wounded may be healed without denying their wounds, and even the fallen remain capable of return.

## I. What Is the Troth?

The word *troth* retains an older meaning that modern words such as loyalty or belief only partially preserve.

Troth is fidelity.

It is faith pledged and faith kept.

It concerns truthfulness between persons, the keeping of word, the maintenance of relation, and the refusal to abandon what has rightly been entrusted.

Yet within *Aru Va'en*, the term must mean more than keeping a promise.

Promises themselves can be wicked.

A guardian may swear fidelity to a corrupt throne.

A people may covenant together around hatred.

A soldier may faithfully execute an unjust command.

A victim may vow never to forgive.

A community may preserve a tradition precisely because generations before it preserved the same tradition.

In every such case, fidelity exists as intensity but may fail as right relation.

Thus the Troth cannot mean simply:

**Keep whatever you have promised to keep.**

It must instead mean something closer to:

> **Remain faithful to the true relation in which every promise, office, identity, and obligation receives its proper meaning.**

This makes the Troth both relational and transcendent.

It exists through relationships, but it cannot be exhausted by any one relationship.

It is embodied in history, but no historical structure owns it.

It calls for fidelity, but it can also demand that an inherited form of fidelity be relinquished.

This is the paradox at the center of the Troth:

> **Sometimes faithfulness requires remaining.  
> Sometimes faithfulness requires leaving.**

## II. Before Throne

The throne signifies authority, continuity, law, office, hierarchy, institutional order, and the center around which a community organizes itself.

These things are not inherently corrupt.

But the throne faces a permanent temptation: to treat the order it administers as the source of the good it was created to serve.

Then the throne no longer says:

**I serve what is true.**

It says:

**What I preserve must be true because I preserve it.**

The Troth stands before the throne because no office can generate its own moral legitimacy.

The king cannot make betrayal faithful by commanding it.

The institution cannot make falsehood true by canonizing it.

The church cannot make domination holy by blessing it.

The state cannot transform sacrifice into righteousness merely by demanding it.

The guardian cannot make the throne worthy through the intensity of its defense.

The Shepherd judges the throne; the confession that the Troth remains denies the throne any power to manufacture the Good.

This means that fidelity to the Troth may sometimes appear as disloyalty to an institution.

But rebellion can become its own idolatry.

Neither submission nor rebellion is inherently faithful.

The Troth stands before both.

## III. Before Pasture

The pasture represents the people, the home, the flock, the shared place of nourishment and belonging.

Guardians frequently justify themselves not by devotion to rulers but by devotion to those they protect.

**I did it for my people.**

**I did it for my family.**

**I did it for the flock.**

Such claims can be sincere.

They can also become morally catastrophic.

A community does not become righteous simply because it is vulnerable.

A people does not acquire unlimited moral claims because it has suffered.

A flock cannot demand that everything beyond the pasture be destroyed in the name of its safety.

The pasture is good because it gives life.

It is not the source of life.

There may therefore come a time when the pasture must be left.

The Shepherd leads.

The pasture does not possess the flock.

Thus the Troth stands before the community as surely as it stands before the throne.

## IV. Before Fang

The fang symbolizes capacity: strength, guardianship, force, vigilance, discernment, courage, and willingness to stand between danger and the vulnerable.

The wolf is not fallen because it has teeth.

The guardian is not guilty because it possesses strength.

Power can serve the Troth.

But fang possesses its own temptation.

The guardian can begin to infer righteousness from the act of guarding.

It says:

**I stood when others fled.**

**I suffered for them.**

**I bore what they could not bear.**

**I remained.**

These statements may all be true.

They still do not answer:

**Was the keeping faithful?**

The Troth breaks this circle.

> **Before fang.**

The guardian cannot say:

**I kept, therefore I was righteous.**

It can only say:

**I kept. Let the keeping be judged.**

This is the beginning of Unclaimed Virtue.

## V. Before Fleece

Fleece represents vulnerability, innocence, dependence, suffering, belonging, and the condition of being guarded.

The fleece also cannot claim virtue.

Defending the wounded does not require declaring the wounded infallible.

Suffering establishes that suffering occurred.

It does not establish that every judgment of the sufferer is correct.

Victimhood can become identity.

Identity can become authority.

Authority can become a new throne.

A wounded community may demand vengeance.

A dependent flock may demand permanent guardianship.

A vulnerable people may outsource violence while preserving an image of innocence.

The Living Lamb may say:

**We did not bear the fang. Therefore we bear no responsibility for what the fang did for us.**

The Troth refuses this evasion.

Strength does not confer righteousness.

Weakness does not confer righteousness.

Guarding does not confer righteousness.

Being guarded does not confer righteousness.

Wounding does not confer righteousness.

Suffering does not confer righteousness.

Fang and fleece describe conditions within relationship.

They do not deliver final moral verdicts.

## VI. The Troth and Unclaimed Virtue

The Doctrine of Unclaimed Virtue follows from this structure.

If neither throne, pasture, fang, nor fleece can establish righteousness, then no creature possesses a secure position from which it may canonize itself.

This does not require moral relativism.

Some acts are faithful.

Some acts are wicked.

The refusal to claim virtue means refusing to convert one's participation in the good into ownership of goodness.

Virtue remains unclaimed because final judgment remains beyond the self.

This provides freedom.

The creature no longer needs to prove its own goodness in order to continue acting.

It can confess error without collapsing.

It can receive praise without converting praise into identity.

It can discover that an earlier act of keeping was misguided without concluding that its entire life was meaningless.

Unclaimed Virtue is a refusal of moral self-sovereignty.

## VII. The Troth and Apostasy

Apostasy is commonly imagined as abandonment.

But *Aru Va'en* identifies a more difficult possibility:

> **Apostasy can occur through fidelity itself.**

A guardian continues guarding after the object of guardianship has ceased serving the good.

A community preserves a structure after that structure has become destructive.

An oath continues to be obeyed after obedience has become betrayal.

A people repeats a sacrifice because admitting its futility would threaten the meaning of previous sacrifices.

The person appears faithful precisely because the external form of fidelity remains intact.

The Troth distinguishes **fidelity to form** from **fidelity through form**.

Forms matter.

Promises matter.

Institutions matter.

Rituals matter.

Memory matters.

But none is ultimate.

When the form begins destroying the relation it was meant to embody, preservation of the form can become betrayal of the Troth.

Sometimes fidelity must break the visible shape of yesterday's fidelity.

## VIII. The Troth and Repentance

If fidelity were identical with perfect continuity, repentance would always appear as failure.

To repent is to admit:

**What I was doing must change.**

If the Troth is deeper than the guardian's historical form, repentance may itself become fidelity.

The guardian can say:

**I was wrong about what faithfulness required.**

without saying:

**Faithfulness was meaningless.**

Thus:

> **Even for the fallen, the Troth remains.**

This statement does not erase judgment.

It makes judgment capable of leading somewhere other than annihilation.

## IX. The Troth and Memory

Communities require memory.

Guardians depend upon it.

The Aruvan can be understood as embodied accumulated memory: generations of keeping taking form.

But memory carries danger.

Past fidelity can become precedent.

Precedent can become identity.

Identity can become prohibition:

**We cannot change because changing would dishonor those who kept before us.**

Past sacrifice becomes a debt that can never be discharged.

The Troth refuses to make memory sovereign.

The dead are honored by truth, not by compulsory repetition.

A sacrifice does not become righteous because another sacrifice preceded it.

A mistake does not become sacred through age.

Memory serves the Troth when it enables more faithful discernment.

## X. The Troth and the Shepherd

Troth language is not self-interpreting.

If every guardian simply claims direct access to the Troth, the language can become an excuse for unlimited private certainty.

The Shepherd is the measure that prevents Troth language from becoming a weapon of self-certification.

Within the Christian grammar of *Aru Va'en*, the Shepherd is Jesus Christ, the living disclosure and enactment of the divine fidelity that Troth language attempts to name.

He keeps without possession.

He exercises authority without requiring victims to sustain his throne.

He judges without making judgment annihilation.

He receives the fallen without denying the wound.

He strengthens the guarded rather than cultivating permanent dependence.

The Shepherd does not answer to the Troth as though it were a second authority.

Troth language answers to him. It names, constructively and dependently, the fidelity revealed in his life, cross, resurrection, judgment, mercy, and call to creaturely participation.

## XI. The Troth Is Not Neutrality

Because the Troth relativizes all roles, it could be misunderstood as perpetual hesitation.

But the Troth is not neutrality.

Fidelity demands action.

The guardian may need to stand.

The Living Lamb may need to speak.

The Vaerun may need to cross.

The Hrovan may need to preserve.

The community may need to exclude a real danger.

Unclaimed Virtue does not mean unclaimed responsibility.

The ethic is demanding:

> **Act faithfully without possessing certainty that your faithfulness makes you good.**

Discern.

Commit.

Keep.

Release.

Repent when necessary.

Repair what can be repaired.

But do not make the act itself a throne.

## XII. The Troth and Truth

The language of fidelity becomes sentimental unless ordered toward truth.

A guardian may protect someone from consequences that person needs to face.

A community may preserve peace by suppressing testimony about harm.

A friend may call silence loyalty.

A church may call concealment unity.

The Troth therefore cannot mean mere relational continuity.

The relationship must remain ordered toward truth.

Fidelity without truth becomes complicity.

Truth without fidelity can become accusation without mercy.

The Shepherd holds both.

## XIII. The Troth and Mercy

If truth alone defined the Troth, the system could become an architecture of endless judgment.

Mercy does not contradict truth.

Mercy refuses to reduce a creature to the truth of its worst act.

This allows the fallen guardian to remain more than its fall.

It allows the wounded Living Lamb to remain more than its wound.

Truth names what has been.

Mercy refuses to declare that what has been exhausts what may become.

This is why the Troth remains even for the fallen.

## XIV. The Troth as Relation Rather Than Possession

The throne tries to possess authority.

The pasture tries to possess belonging.

The fang tries to possess responsibility.

The fleece tries to possess innocence.

The institution tries to possess truth.

The victim may try to possess judgment.

The penitent may even try to possess redemption.

The Troth permits none of these.

Faithfulness exists only in relation.

It cannot be accumulated like property.

Yesterday's fidelity cannot simply be spent to justify today's action.

One does not own the Troth.

One participates in it.

And because participation is relational, no participant can possess the whole.

This is also why nothing faithful keeps alone.

## XV. The Troth Remains

The confession that **the Troth remains** is not a claim that nothing changes.

Almost everything may change.

Thrones fall.

Pastures are left.

Guardians relinquish their office.

Lambs mature.

Institutions repent.

Communities divide and reconcile.

The forms through which fidelity once became visible may disappear entirely.

The Troth remains because it was never identical with those forms.

It remembers without worshiping memory.

It keeps without possessing.

It changes without becoming arbitrary.

It judges without abandoning.

It forgives without denying.

It restores without pretending there was no wound.

Thus the confession:

> **Before throne and pasture.  
> Before fang and fleece.  
> The Troth remains.**

is not primarily a celebration of endurance.

It is a declaration of priority.

Before authority.

Before community.

Before strength.

Before vulnerability.

Before the categories by which creatures divide themselves into keeper and kept.

There is a fidelity none of them creates and none of them possesses.

The guardian may fail it.

The Living Lamb may distort it.

The throne may claim it.

The pasture may confuse itself with it.

Still:

**The Troth remains.**

And because it remains, repentance does not require the destruction of the self.

Because it remains, the fallen are not abandoned to the moment of their fall.

Because it remains, the wounded need not make the wound eternal in order for the wound to be remembered.

Because it remains, the guardian can finally relinquish what it cannot faithfully keep.

Because it remains, no institution has the power to declare itself indispensable.

Because it remains, neither fang nor fleece bears the final word.

The Troth is not what creatures possess when they have proven themselves faithful.

It is the relation into which they are continually called.

Its judgment strips away the claim to virtue.

Its mercy leaves open the path of return.

Its endurance makes reconciliation possible.

And its final confession remains the first:

**Before throne and pasture.  
Before fang and fleece.  
The Troth remains.**
